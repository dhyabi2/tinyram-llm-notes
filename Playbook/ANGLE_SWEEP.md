# Angle sweep: gaps between what the hardware can do and what our system achieves

Eight independent angles, 56 candidate ideas, the top two per angle (16) attacked by a skeptic agent. Every skeptic verdict was 'weakened' (none confirmed, none refuted): the skeptics were told to default to weakened/refuted unless they could confirm the numbers, so read 'weakened' as 'bound lowered', and read the corrected bound as the planning number. 40 ideas were not verified (see the list). All speedups are x over our current pool path on the Mac proxy unless stated; derivations are in each idea's entry and in the agents' scratch folders. Nothing here is measured end to end unless stated; measured items are in the vault (M20-M24).

## Cross-angle clusters (the same finding reached from several angles)

1. **Overlap reads with compute, hits first, blocking I/O threads** (hardware, pipeline, policy, os, redteam, layout): proposed from 6 angles; corrected bounds 1.25-1.5x; no prediction needed; risk: thread-spin barriers (8-thread collapse) so I/O threads must block.
2. **Make the dense (non-expert) weights resident** (redteam, os, bytes): 0.06-0.07 GiB/token still pages through mmap at 0.28-0.6 GB/s; corrected bound 1.2-1.3x. First test in progress (LLAMA_MLOCK_DENSE).
3. **AVX512-VNNI Q4_K/Q6_K dot kernel** (hardware): microbench 1.79x on the kernel; 1.17x in the serial pool regime, up to 1.55x once reads are hidden. Not yet verified by a skeptic.
4. **Cold-start bias in all our short runs** (redteam): the 3.46 tok/s pool figure is a cold 16-token number; steady state after warm-up is ~5 tok/s (estimate); the 5x claim is mildly biased at 1500 MB.
5. **Baseline strength** (redteam): the 5x is against stock macOS mmap; mmap + per-expert WILLNEED/F_RDADVISE was never run on the Mac; a published pool on a 73.5 GB model got ~1.5x.

## Red-team findings on our own headlines (condensed from the redteam angle)

RED-TEAM FINDINGS (ranked by effect on headlines). All sim numbers are mine, from tinyram-llm data/traces (Qwen3-30B, 128 experts top-8, 48 layers, 384 entries/token, about 2.65 MB/entry); scratch at <scratch> and e.py.

1. The 3.46 tok/s pool figure (M23) is a cold-start number. LRU on the real traces from an empty cache gives hit 0.66/0.71/0.66 over the first 16 tokens at a 2264-entry pool (about 6 GB), matching the measured 66%. The same cache after warm-up gives 0.95/0.98/0.91. Misses fall from about 0.34 to about 0.05 of 384 entries, so read time falls from about 0.11 s to about 0.02 s per token. Compute plus sync is about 0.18 s, so steady state is about 5 tok/s (estimate), roughly 1.45x above what we quote. The side effect is that every 8-16 token run understates long-generation speed, and the cold-start term also contaminates the 5x (M22). At the 1500 MB pool (566 entries) cold8 is 0.31/0.42/0.37 and steady is 0.43-0.48, so the 5x there is only mildly biased. This also means M22/M23 hit rates are not inflated by llama-bench's fixed synthetic input (L07 worry): the measured 66% equals the real-trace cold-16 sim.

2. Time budget of the 5x run does not add up unless the residual mmap paging is large. M22 token time is 1/1.72 = 0.58 s. Compute is about 0.18 s (M23). Pool misses at 566 entries and 45% hit are about 0.55 x 1.0 GB = 0.55 GB, about 0.18 s at 3.1 GB/s. The remaining about 0.2 s matches 0.065 GiB/token still paged through mmap at 0.28-0.6 GB/s. That is a third of the token time spent in the slow path we claim to have replaced (estimate; needs per-tensor mincore).

3. The 5x baseline is stock macOS mmap, a weak baseline. Combined effect of read size, policy and pool is credited to the pool. The fair comparison (pool vs mmap plus per-expert MADV_WILLNEED/F_RDADVISE at 2.7 MB) was never run on the Mac. Linux readahead and real small-RAM PCs behave differently (VPS: 0.04-0.18 tok/s, M18/M20). Prior RFC data: 0.43 -> 0.66 t/s from a persistent read pool (about 1.5x) on a 73.5 GB model with 32 GB RAM, much less than 5x (https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/discussions/23324). The 5x is a ballast-regime, macOS-stock figure; expect 2-5x elsewhere. Ballast realism: a wired 9 GB ballast is not compressible, not swappable and leaves no competing page cache; a real 8 GB PC runs a browser and OS caches (M21 notes swap +1 GB). The 10 GB ballast failed to wire, so the cap curve has one valid point.

4. madvise 1.65x (M20): one throttled VPS disk at 120 MiB/s, one prompt, n=5, 2 threads, fully I/O-bound. Null pair is clean (0.2%), so it is real for that regime, but the gain is I/O-share dependent; on a 3 GB/s NVMe with 0.18 s compute the same prefetch should give much less, and the Mac prefetch A/B at ballast is still absent. Do not carry 1.65x to the Mac or to the 125B.

5. Hit curves: the 125B has 512 experts, top-10, so footprint is 480 entries (about 1.3 GB), not 384, and reuse is lower (public benchmark: 0.77 at 16 GB vs our 0.95). The combination table D is evaluated at the 0.95 point, where overlap looks like 1.03-1.04x. At hit 0.77 and 16 GB: io = 0.23 x 1.30 GB / 3.1 = 0.096 s, compute 1/4.58 = 0.218 s, serial 0.314 s -> overlap 0.218 s = 1.44x. So the lever ranking changes with the hit curve: overlap and prefetch were dismissed on the wrong hit rate. Cross-layer prefetch 1.00-1.075x was also measured in the 0.95 regime. A RFC commenter measured 1.30x from pipelining next-layer misses at 75% hit on an M1 Air CPU.

6. Reading the compute ceiling as compute is likely wrong. 30B: 1.86 GB touched x 8.3 tok/s = 15.4 GB/s. 125B: 3.23 GB x 4.57 = 14.8 GB/s. Two models, same 15 GB/s, while 55-75 GFLOP/s is a small fraction of AVX512 VNNI peak. That is a DRAM-streaming ceiling (LPDDR4X-3733 4x16 peak is about 58 GB/s, to be measured). Hardware can do more than software achieves; nobody tested a read-bandwidth microbenchmark.

7. Quality gates: top-6 +4.7% and top-4 +30.9% on Qwen3-30B (few chunks, plain renormalisation) vs OLMoE +8.8/+45.6% vs a 397B paper with anchored denominator 0.55 MMLU at 10->5. Three different answers; none on the 125B. Measured on few chunks, so confidence intervals are not stated. Cheapest decisive test: same perplexity run on 5+ chunk sets with bootstrap CI plus anchored denominator; then a downstream task (GSM8K 100 items).

8. Order effects: M22 notes both arms trend upward across pairs (OS state), and 3 pairs only. Use ABBA with a null (pool vs pool) pair and an n >= 5, 32+ token real-prompt run. Single model, single prompt family: traces db/story/code are 249-299 tokens each; cross-prompt cold warm-start sim gives first-16 hit +0 to +0.2 depending on cache size.

DISMISSED TOO EARLY or mis-scored: (a) overlap/prefetch lever (scored at hit 0.95; at 0.77 it is 1.3-1.44x); (b) pool size under ballast (never swept under ballast; M23 was unballasted); (c) prefill path (pool measured on decode only; prefill touches 68-93% of experts, so a 69 GB file is read once per prompt); (d) 8-thread collapse treated as fixed (it is spin barriers, SMT might give 5-15%).

CONFIRMED DEAD (with my numbers): batching independent sequences to share expert reads: union of 2 and 3 different-prompt tokens is 362 and 343 entries per token vs 384 (only 6-11% sharing) and LRU hit falls from 0.46 to 0.05 at 566 entries and 0.69 to 0.50-0.61 at 1130; lossless entropy coding of Q4_K expert bytes: 8 MB samples of OLMoE Q4_K_M give zlib 0.981-0.998, lzma 0.987-1.000, byte entropy 7.80-7.93 bits, nibble entropy 3.91-3.97 bits; the I/O saving is at most about 2% (consistent with EntroLLM's note that block-wise quantization has high entropy).

SOURCES READ: llama.cpp RFC #23324 (disk-paged MoE pool, M-series data and CPU comments) and DALI (arxiv 2602.03495; workload-aware placement, residual prefetch only +9%, GPU-based). Others searched: HOBBIT (2411.01433), ProMoE (2410.22134), MoESD (2505.19645), SpecMoE (2604.10152), EntroLLM (2505.02380), LLM in a flash (2312.11514), SSD offload energy (2508.06978).


## Angle: hardware

Reads already run at the hardware limit (3.11 GB/s explicit vs a ~3 GB/s-class internal NVMe), so the remaining hardware gaps are in compute and in serialisation. I measured them with small C microbenchmarks. They ran on a shared machine with about 4 cores of other agents' load, so treat the numbers as lower bounds.

(1) The decode dot kernel is the main gap. The local llama.cpp build uses -march=native, which defines __AVX512VNNI__. The Q4_K x Q8_K dot in ggml/src/ggml-cpu/arch/x86/quants.c (about line 2057) is still 256-bit AVX2, using maddubs, madd and add. A Q4_K-like AVX2 loop streams 18.8 GB/s of weights from DRAM with 4 threads. A 512-bit VNNI loop (vpdpbusd) reaches 33.7 GB/s, which is 1.79x. Raw 4-thread DRAM read is 42.0 GB/s (spec peak for LPDDR4X-3733 128-bit is ~59.7 GB/s, an estimate). Cached decode needs 8.3 tok/s x ~1.86 GB per token (3.3B active x 4.5 bits, an estimate) = 15.4 GB/s. That matches the AVX2 microbenchmark, so decode is kernel-bound, not DRAM-bound, at about 37% of DRAM bandwidth. This is consistent with M16, where Q2_K reads 40% fewer bytes and gains nothing.

(2) The proxy runs use --repack 0 (Fixes/F01), so they cannot use the repacked AVX2 8x8 path. That path needs a RAM-resident copy and is also AVX2 only.

(3) With 4 threads the machine can also stream 42 GB/s of DRAM. With 8 threads DRAM streaming falls to 36.4 GB/s, which agrees with the 8-thread collapse in M23.

(4) Rejected by measurement: lossless entropy coding of the expert bytes. Order-0 entropy of the Q4_K nibbles is 3.90 bits per 4, and zlib-9 and lzma both reach 0.978 of the original size. That saves 2.2-2.6%, so no disk-side compression or compressed-memory trick is worth building. The iGPU is also not an obvious win, because it shares the same ~42 GB/s of DRAM and the same package power as the CPU.

Ranking by bound x plausibility: 1 (VNNI kernels), 2 (overlap with blocking I/O threads), 3 (barrier, poll and QoS), 5 (expert bundling), 4 (second NVMe), 6 (bandwidth-matched core split), 7 (iGPU), 8 (entropy coding, rejected).

Sources read properly: the local ggml x86 Q4_K dot source and repack source, and FlashMoE (arXiv 2601.17063). FlashMoE reports over 70% of decode time spent in SSD expert loads, a ~3 ms SSD load versus ~158 us of FFN compute per expert, and claims full FFN overlap, but gives no thread, kernel or prefetch details. Web searches found no published Q4_K AVX512-VNNI decode benchmark and no llama.cpp numbers on a Gen11 Iris iGPU.

### hardware #1: Stripe expert reads across a second NVMe (second M.2 slot or Thunderbolt 3 enclosure)
- **Gap:** Internal NVMe is at its limit at 3.11 GB/s (M15) and reads cost 0.11 s per token (M23). An internal drive alone cannot go faster. A second PCIe 3.0 x4 device via TB3 (practical about 2.5-2.8 GB/s, estimate) is idle hardware. The Playbook INVESTIGATION notes already mention a claim that 3x needs two NVMe stripes.
- **Change:** Keep half of the experts (or a full copy) on the second drive and let the read pool issue each miss to the drive that holds it, with one queue per device.
- **Bound:** Read bandwidth 3.11 + about 2.7 = 5.8 GB/s (estimate), so reads fall from 0.11 s to 0.059 s per token. Serial regime: 0.29 to 0.239, so 1.21x. Read-bound regime (after overlap): 1.8x capped by the compute floor of 0.18 s (1.0x beyond that), so it matters only if compute is first made faster (ideas 1 and 2).
- **Cheapest test:** tools/read_bench.py: read from two files on two devices concurrently (any external SSD) and check the sum. Hardware needed, so cost is hardware.
- **Prior art:** Multi-SSD striping is standard in storage; for LLMs, flash-array offload systems exist for GPU servers. Not found for a 16 GB laptop with an external drive; the vault only mentions it as a claim.
- **Skeptic (weakened, corrected 1.20x):** Local files plus two searches. I ran no benchmark.

1. The 1.8x is a read-bound-regime number that does not apply today. M23 measures 0.29 s/token, of which reads are only about 0.11 s (3.46 tok/s, 66% hit, 353 MiB/token). Reads and compute are serial today.

2. Serial arithmetic on the Qwen3-30B proxy:
- Idea's own bandwidth: 3.11 GB/s + 2.7 GB/s = 5.8 GB/s. Reads fall from 0.11 s to 0.11 x 3.11/5.8 = 0.059 s, so the token goes from 0.29 s to 0.239 s. That is 1.21x.
- A realistic TB3 rate is about 2.4-2.5 GB/s. The search found vendors quoting about 2.4-2.5 GB/s sequential and one user measuring 1.55 GB/s. The 2.75 GB/s figure is an uncited forum post. Some TB3 hosts also wire only PCIe x2.
- With 2.45 GB/s, combined bandwidth is 5.56 GB/s and reads fall to 0.062 s. The token goes to 0.242 s, which is 1.20x.

3. Double counting. M15 or later measured the internal drive at 4.14 GB/s with 16 reads in flight. So 3.11 GB/s is not a drive ceiling. A software-only fix (deeper queue) cuts reads to 0.083 s, which is 1.10x with no hardware. A second drive's increment on top of that is only about 4.14 to 6.6 GB/s: the token goes from 0.263 s to 0.235 s, another 1.12x. Most of the idea's headline gain overlaps a free change.

4. Overlapped regime. Once reads overlap compute, the floor is compute, about 0.18-0.22 s. The proxy's compute ceiling is about 9 tok/s from the 55 GFLOP/s decode ceiling, but the practical floor is 0.18 s. Reads are already 0.11 s, which is below 0.18 s, so a faster read path adds 1.0x. The proposal's own text concedes this. It would matter only if compute is first made faster.

5. Target regime. For the 125B model at 16 GB, using a hit rate of 0.77:
- Active weights are about 6B x 0.54 B/param, which is about 3.2 GB/token. Misses are 23%, or about 0.75 GB/token.
- That is 0.24 s at 3.11 GB/s, against compute of about 0.22 s (the 4.58 tok/s ceiling).
- Serial: 0.46 s falls to 0.35 s with 5.6 GB/s (about 1.3x). Overlapped: the cap is 4.58 tok/s, so about 1.1x over a well-overlapped single drive.
- This is the only regime where a second drive helps materially, and it is an estimate, not a measurement.

6. Prior art. A vault note rated a similar "two striped NVMe" idea as confirmed and nothing beyond striping. It also cites an independent source (Playbook papers) that saw sub-linear SSD scaling: 1 to 2 drives gave 1.40x, not 2x. Applying 1.40x to the read side gives about 1.1x serial. Published MoE SSD-offload work (arXiv 2508.06978 energy study; FlashMoE 2601.17063 cache policy; Flash-MoE community project) does not report multi-drive scaling, so there is no support for 1.8x.

7. Other penalties. TB3 adds latency to each 2.7 MB read. Whole-expert placement across two drives has 24-27% load imbalance (vault note), which erodes the combined bandwidth further. Hardware cost and a portability loss: the result needs an external drive. Quality risk is none.

Net: 1.2x serial on the proxy (1.10-1.12x if the free queue-depth gain is taken first), about 1.0x once overlapped, and at most about 1.3x serial / 1.1x overlapped on the 125B target. The 1.8x is not supported.
  - missed prior art: 
  - decisive test: First, on the internal drive, sweep reads in flight 4/8/16/32 with tools/read_bench.py and record GB/s (the vault already shows 4.14 GB/s at 16). Then measure a real TB3 enclosure alone and concurrently with the internal drive on 2.7 MB F_NOCACHE random reads. Accept the idea only if the concurrent sum exceeds the best single-drive GB/s by at least 1.5x and the token is still read-bound after overlap. On the pool, compare tok/s with reads at 16 in flight against 16 in flight plus the second drive on the 125B-class footprint.

### hardware #2: Overlap expert reads with compute using blocking I/O threads and hits-first ordering
- **Gap:** M23: at 6 GB pool, 66% hit, about 353 MiB read per token, 0.11 s of reads in a 0.29 s token (3.46 tok/s); reads and compute are serial today. Cached compute alone is 0.12 s (M10), so about 0.06 s of the token is neither read nor core compute. Reads already hit 3.11 GB/s, the hardware limit, so the only remaining lever is hiding them. M23: 8 threads collapse to 0.258 tok/s, so I/O threads must block, not spin, and the 4 compute threads must stay at 4.
- **Change:** Issue the miss preads for a layer as soon as the router output is known, from 2 dedicated blocking threads (not part of the ggml spin pool), and compute cache-hit experts first so miss compute starts when data lands. Optionally pipeline the next layer's reads with a lossless same-layer-only ordering. No prediction is needed for the hits-first part.
- **Bound:** Full overlap: token = max(read 0.11, other 0.18) = 0.18 s vs 0.29 s, so 1.61x. Hits-first only: per layer read = 0.11/48 = 2.3 ms, other = 0.18/48 = 3.75 ms. Hit-expert compute is about 66% of the expert share, about 1.3 ms (estimate), so up to 1.3 ms of the 2.3 ms read is hidden: (6.05-1.3)/6.05 gives 1.28x. The M23 note's own 6-8 tok/s overlap bound assumes ideal compute.
- **Cheapest test:** Free trace-driven version: use the real traces (data/traces/qwen3-30b_*.txt) to count per-layer misses and hits, then compute the hidden fraction with measured per-layer times from M23. Then a 20-line patch to the pool: issue misses asynchronously and reorder expert indices hits-first in the MUL_MAT_ID loop; compare tok/s at 9 GB ballast against M22's 1.72.
- **Prior art:** FlashMoE (arXiv 2601.17063, read) overlaps cache management and FFN with SSD loads on a discrete-GPU PC at 7.4 GB/s SSD, claiming full FFN overlap. Klotski (arXiv 2502.06888) overlaps via multi-batch pipelines. Expert-prefetch work (arXiv 2603.19289) speculates routing. All are GPU-offload regimes; none gives an intra-layer hits-first schedule for a spin-barrier CPU runtime with blocking I/O threads. Our notes measured lossless cross-layer prefetch at only 1.00-1.075x, so this idea avoids prediction.
- **Skeptic (weakened, corrected 1.25x):** Verdict: weakened. The 1.6x claim does not survive, and a realistic gain is about 1.1-1.25x.

1. The full-overlap bound ignores the layer dependency. Layer L+1 needs layer L's output, and the next router only runs after that. Without prediction, a layer's miss reads can overlap only with that layer's own expert compute, not with attention or other layers. M10 gives cached compute 0.12 s per token, so 2.5 ms per layer. Qwen3-30B's expert share of that is roughly 55% by parameter count (my estimate, not measured), or 1.4 ms per layer. Maximum hidden time is then 48 x 1.4 ms = 0.066 s, so 0.29 -> 0.224 s, which is 1.30x at most. The proposal's max(read 0.11, other 0.18) lets reads hide behind attention and the lm_head, which they cannot.

2. The 66% hit rate is a cold-start artifact. M23's 5997 MiB read over 17 tokens (353 MiB per token) includes the compulsory misses. The 12 GB pool had the same hits as the 6 GB pool, which says the same thing. I ran an LRU of 2000 experts (6 GB) over the real traces (qwen3-30b_db, story and code):
- Over the first 17 tokens the hit rate is 0.66-0.73 and about 300-370 MiB are read per token. That matches M23.
- After 100 tokens of warm-up the hit rate is 0.89-0.96 and only 47-123 MiB are read per token (about 0.015-0.04 s).
- Steady-state decode is therefore much less read-bound than M23 suggests. The overlap gain shrinks accordingly.

3. I modelled a pipelined schedule per layer: hits computed first, then each miss computed as it lands. It assumes 3.1 GB/s reads, 3 MB per expert, and 0.172 ms per expert of compute. The 0.06 s of unexplained token time is held constant.
- Cold (first 17 tokens): 1.16-1.19x.
- Warm: 1.07-1.15x.
- Using the 2-thread read rate of ~2.3 GB/s (estimated from M15, not measured) changes this by only about 0.01x.
- The 0.06 s of unexplained token time is not shown to be hideable.

4. The 3.1 GB/s rate (M15) was measured with 4 reads in flight on an idle machine with no compute running. With 2 I/O threads the aggregate rate is lower. M15 shows 1 thread at 1.99 GB/s for whole experts, and the three tensors of one expert read in parallel behave like 2.7 GB/s. The 0.11 s of reads is computed, not measured.

5. Core contention is a real risk. The current pool has the 4 ggml threads do the preads themselves, after a barrier. Adding 2 I/O threads on 4 physical cores while the compute threads spin-wait is the oversubscription that M23 shows costs 11x at 8 threads. A woken I/O thread may have to wait out a scheduler timeslice. Giving compute 3 threads instead loses about 25% of compute.

6. Gate and up run as separate MUL_MAT_ID ops with barriers, then down. Reads for all three can be issued at router time, but this needs a restructured op, not a "20-line patch". Hits-first only helps the gate/up ops that compute misses last.

7. Prior art contradicts the large gain. In the llama.cpp discussion (https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/discussions/23324), a dedicated I/O thread loads the next layer's misses while the main thread computes the current layer's hits, in a pipelined CPU benchmark. It was measured on Qwen3-30B-A3B in f16 on an M1 Air (16 GB), where per-layer compute is 4.7 ms, much larger than ours:
- 1.09x at 50% hit, 1.30x at 75% hit and 1.18x at 88% hit.
- No gain at 0% or 100% hit.
- In the same thread, a CUDA test with overlapped reads moved throughput only from 0.66 to 0.68 tok/s because it was bytes-per-token bound.

FlashMoE (https://arxiv.org/html/2601.17063v1) is a discrete-GPU regime. Its 2.6x comes mainly from better cache replacement (up to 51% higher hit rate than LRU/LFU), not from overlap. I did not fetch its full text.

8. For the 125B target, the 4.58 tok/s compute ceiling and a ~0.77 hit rate mean reads are a larger share of the token, so overlap matters more there. But the same per-layer cap applies: the hidden time is at most the expert compute per layer.

The proposal's 1.28x for hits-first alone happens to be close to my corrected bound. The 1.6x headline is not supported. The proposal's own note that no prediction is needed is correct.
  - missed prior art: 
  - decisive test: Run the existing patched llama-bench at 4 threads, no ballast, with the pool at 6 GB. Use at least 64 tokens and discard the first 32, so the cold-start misses are excluded. Log the per-token misses. Run it twice: once as the baseline, and once with a cheap hack that issues all of a layer's miss preads from 2 blocking threads at router time, with the gate/up/down loads still barriered but overlapped with hit compute. Compare steady-state tok/s. Since the bound is 1.07-1.25x, gains under 1.08x fail the null test. A free pre-check is the LRU simulation, with the scratch script at <scratch> A second pre-check is the existing phase-timing patch (llama-phase-timing.patch), which can measure the real read time per token in steady state and the real expert share of compute, replacing my estimated 55%.

### hardware #3: AVX512-VNNI (zmm vpdpbusd) Q4_K and Q6_K expert dot kernels for decode
- **Gap:** Q4_K x Q8_K dot is 256-bit AVX2 maddubs+madd+add even though the build defines __AVX512VNNI__ (ggml/src/ggml-cpu/arch/x86/quants.c ~line 2057). Microbench, 4 threads, weights from DRAM: AVX2 Q4_K-like loop 18.8 GB/s vs zmm VNNI loop 33.7 GB/s (1.79x); L2-resident 25.1 vs 46.9 GB/s (1.87x); raw DRAM read 42.0 GB/s. Real decode (M10) 8.3 tok/s x ~1.86 GB = 15.4 GB/s, about 82% of the AVX2 loop. Scratch: <scratch> M16: Q2_K reads 40% fewer bytes with no decode speedup, so decode is compute-bound.
- **Change:** Add an AVX512BW/VNNI path for ggml_vec_dot_q4_K_q8_K and q6_K_q8_K (and the MUL_MAT_ID call path). Use vpdpbusd per 32-weight sub-block into int32 lanes, apply the 6-bit scales and mins after the dot, and keep the AVX2 path as fallback. Output must stay near-identical; integer sums are exact, so identity tests should pass.
- **Bound:** Kernel speedup 33.7/18.8 = 1.79x (microbench omits scale handling, so a real kernel is maybe 1.4-1.6x; estimate). Assume 80% of cached token time is the dot kernel (estimate) and 0.12 s per cached token (1/8.3). Token = 0.12 x (0.2 + 0.8/1.79) = 0.0774 s, so 1.55x cached (about 12.9 tok/s). In the serial pool regime (0.29 s per token, 66% hit) only the compute part shrinks: 0.29 - 0.0426 = 0.247 s, so 1.17x. With reads fully overlapped (idea 2) the compute side shrinks to 0.0774 and the token becomes read-bound at about 0.11 s (about 9 tok/s).
- **Cheapest test:** Standalone: write the VNNI Q4_K x Q8_K dot as a C file in the scratchpad, check bit-exactness against the existing AVX2 function on random blocks, and time both over 512 MB with 4 threads. Pass if at least 1.4x. Then, when running models is allowed, llama-bench -t 4 on the cached 30B model.
- **Prior art:** VNNI already exists in ggml for Q4_0/Q8_0 paths (ggml-quants dpbusd helper in mul_sum_i8_pairs_float with maddubs fallback) and in the x86 repack GEMM at arch/x86/repack.cpp (AVX512VNNI at about lines 124 and 152). The web search found no published Q4_K decode benchmark with AVX512-VNNI on a client Ice Lake part, and the commit history says Q4_K AVX2 mattered mainly for perplexity passes. Regime: the published VNNI paths are mostly prompt processing on newer CPUs.

### hardware #4: Barrier cost: poll level, thread priority/QoS and straggler sensitivity of the ggml threadpool
- **Gap:** M23: 4 threads 2.98 tok/s, 2 threads 2.74 (doubling cores gives only 1.09x), 8 threads 0.258 (11.6x collapse); the note blames spinning barriers. The microbench scales 1 to 4 threads at 2.9x on DRAM (14.6 to 42.0 GB/s) and 8 threads is worse (36.4 GB/s). Right now the machine has about 4 cores of background load (load average 3.5, other processes at 150% CPU), and a descheduled thread stalls all spinning peers at every barrier.
- **Change:** Test llama.cpp's own --poll (0-100), --prio (0-3) and cpu-strict options; on macOS also set thread QoS to USER_INTERACTIVE and a thread-affinity tag. Longer term: hybrid spin-then-block barrier (spin about 50 us, then os_sync_wait_on_address or ulock) so a straggler does not burn the sibling hyperthread.
- **Bound:** Estimate only: we have no barrier count per token. If 2 to 4 threads is ideal 2x but achieves 1.09x on the read-light parts, the ceiling is far above 1.3. A conservative 1.3x assumes 23% of the token is straggler/barrier wait under background load. The 11.6x collapse at 8 threads shows the penalty per straggler is very large, so the true bound under load could be higher.
- **Cheapest test:** With a fully cached model: llama-bench -t 4 with --poll 0, 50, 100 and --prio 0 vs 2, once on an idle machine and once with one competing busy process (yes > /dev/null). If tok/s with one competitor drops by more than 25%, stragglers are real and the hybrid barrier is worth building.
- **Prior art:** llama.cpp exposes --poll and --prio for exactly this (threadpool poll level and priority). Spin-then-block barriers are standard (OpenMP GOMP_SPINCOUNT, Linux futex). No macOS-specific MoE-from-disk measurements found; our notes only record the 8-thread collapse.

### hardware #5: Bundle each expert's gate, up and down into one contiguous extent in a re-laid-out GGUF
- **Gap:** M15: 0.9 MB reads at 4 threads give 2.69 GB/s, 2.7 MB (one whole expert) 3.11 GB/s, 4.0 MB 3.42 GB/s. In GGUF each expert's three tensors sit in three different regions, so a pool miss is three 0.9 MB reads (M15 says this behaves like the 0.9 MB x 4-thread row, 2.7 GB/s). Reads are 0.11 s per token at 3.11 GB/s.
- **Change:** Offline: rewrite expert tensors so gate/up/down for the same expert are adjacent (and optionally two neighbouring experts, giving about 5 MB extents), plus a small index. The pool then issues one pread per miss, 4 in flight.
- **Bound:** Read rate 2.69 to 3.11 GB/s (+15%) for single-expert bundles, to 3.42 GB/s (+27%) for 4 MB extents. Serial regime: reads 0.11 s become 0.0955 s at 3.11 (from a 2.7 base) or 0.087 s at 3.42; token 0.29 to 0.265, so 1.09x. If the token is read-bound (after overlap, idea 2), the bound is the full 1.27x.
- **Cheapest test:** tools/read_bench.py already measures extents: compare three scattered 0.9 MB preads at 4 threads against one 2.7 MB pread, on a file of 18 GB, with F_NOCACHE. No model run needed. Pass if at least 3.0 GB/s for the three-in-one case under 4 concurrent misses.
- **Prior art:** Row-column bundling in 'LLM in a flash' (Apple, arXiv 2312.11514) reads bundled neurons as larger contiguous chunks to raise flash throughput; regime: smartphone-class flash with FFN neurons, not MoE experts on a laptop.

### hardware #6: Run part of the expert matmuls on the idle Iris Plus G7 (Gen11) iGPU
- **Gap:** iGPU (64 EU, about 1.1 TFLOPS fp32, estimate) is idle. The CPU decode kernel uses 18.8 of 42.0 GB/s DRAM (45%), so there is memory headroom today; after idea 1 the CPU is at 33.7 of 42.0 GB/s and headroom is only 8 GB/s. The iGPU shares the same LPDDR4X and the same 28 W package budget.
- **Change:** A Metal or Vulkan (MoltenVK) backend taking a fixed share of each layer's experts in unified memory, reading the RAM pool directly with no copy.
- **Bound:** With the AVX2 kernel: DRAM-limited ceiling 42/18.8 = 2.2x, but idea 1 already gets 1.79x for free. After idea 1 the gain is capped at 42.0/33.7 = 1.25x on decode. Package power sharing likely cuts CPU clocks, so the real gain is probably below that. For prefill, CPU already reaches 279-412 GFLOP/s (M16) against about 1 TFLOPS iGPU peak, with no simdgroup matrix support on Gen11 (estimate), so gain is small.
- **Cheapest test:** A 50-line Metal microbench: dot of Q4 nibbles against int8 activations over 256 MB while the CPU runs mb.c concurrently, to see whether total bytes/s exceeds 42 GB/s. If the sum is under 45 GB/s, stop.
- **Prior art:** Web searches found no llama.cpp benchmark on Intel Gen11 iGPUs; docs point Intel iGPUs to Vulkan/SYCL, and Metal is aimed at Apple Silicon and discrete AMD. Unified-memory iGPU offload exists on Apple Silicon (Metal backend), where token generation gains are small because decode is memory-bound.

### hardware #7: Bandwidth-matched core split: after the VNNI kernel 3 compute threads may saturate DRAM, freeing a core for I/O and bookkeeping
- **Gap:** Microbench: 1 thread reads DRAM at 14.6 GB/s and 4 threads at 42.0 GB/s (2.9x scaling); VNNI kernel 1 thread 11.3 GB/s, 4 threads 33.7 GB/s. Pool runs always use all 4 cores for compute while pread I/O, the LRU bookkeeping and the kernel's DMA completion contend with them (M23: 3.46 tok/s, 0.29 s per token, 0.06 s unexplained).
- **Change:** After idea 1, run 3 compute threads plus 1 I/O/management thread (4 cores total, no hyperthreads) and measure whether tok/s falls less than the I/O stall removed.
- **Bound:** Estimate: the 0.06 s of unexplained time per token (0.29 - 0.11 - 0.12) is partly I/O-thread interference; removing half of it gives 0.29 to 0.26, 1.1x. If 3 threads cannot saturate DRAM, the 25% fewer compute threads cost more than they save.
- **Cheapest test:** Extend mb.c to nt=3 with the VNNI mode: if 3 threads reach at least 90% of the 4-thread 33.7 GB/s, proceed; otherwise drop the idea.
- **Prior art:** Dedicated I/O cores are standard in storage engines; not found for llama.cpp CPU MoE-from-disk.

### hardware #8: Lossless entropy coding of stored experts to cut bytes read (null test, rejected)
- **Gap:** Reads cost 0.11 s per token; fewer bytes on disk would cut that proportionally. Measured on 3 slices of Qwen3-30B Q4_K_M (14000 blocks each): nibble entropy 3.90 bits of 4, block-header entropy 7.7 of 8 bits; order-0 estimated ratio 0.974, zlib-9 0.978-0.979, lzma 0.978-0.979 (scratchpad ent.py).
- **Change:** Store experts entropy-coded and decode on read (ANS or Huffman); the decode would need to run at 3 GB/s.
- **Bound:** Read bytes shrink by at most 2.6% (order-0 ratio 0.974). Serial regime: 0.11 s x 0.026 = 0.003 s of 0.29 s gives 1.01x; read-bound gives 1.027x, and the decode cost on CPU would exceed that.
- **Cheapest test:** Already done: ent.py on the GGUF; the result is the null.
- **Prior art:** Lossless coding of bf16 weights exists (DFloat11, ZipNN); it works because the exponent has low entropy, which 4-bit block quantisation does not have. Regime: 16-bit weights, not Q4_K.

### policy #1: Hidden-state next-layer router prefetch (replace the id-table predictor) with reads hidden under per-layer compute
- **Gap:** Notes: id-only cross-layer prefetch gives 1.00-1.07x (held-out precision .31-.43). This session confirmed on traces: recall of true layer-l+1 set from layer-l ids = .38@8, .54@16 (hot-set .42/.61). Literature hidden-state predictor: ~.90 recall on Qwen3-30B. Measured: reads are 0.11 s of 0.289 s token (Mac) and 0.096 of 0.314 s (125B 16 GB); per-layer read 2.3 ms (Mac) / 2.0 ms (125B 16 GB) is below per-layer compute 3.75 / 4.5 ms, so a one-layer lead is long enough.
- **Change:** At layer l, after attention, apply layer l+1's norm and router matrix to the current residual (a 2048x128 matvec, microseconds), take top-8 plus a few extra, and issue explicit pool reads for those not resident while layer l's experts compute. True routing still runs at l+1, so output is unchanged (prefetch only). Cap prefetch at the idle-I/O budget and keep reading in the pool's threads (4 threads best, 8 collapse).
- **Bound:** Mac: serial 0.18+0.11=0.289 s. With recall .9, 90% of misses hidden: t = 0.18 + 0.1*0.11 = 0.191 s -> 1.51x (perfect oracle 0.18 s -> 1.61x). 125B 16 GB: 0.314 -> 0.218+0.0096 = 0.228 -> 1.38x. 125B 8 GB (real curve 0.427 s): 0.218+0.1*0.209 = 0.239 -> 1.79x but per-layer read 4.4 ms ~ compute 4.5 ms, so bandwidth-saturated, treat as ceiling. Haircut for wasted prefetch bytes and CPU/memory-bandwidth contention (Flash-MoE reported prefetch net loss on Apple silicon, unverified here) is why plausibility is only ~0.5; also the bound holds only if reads cost no compute cores.
- **Cheapest test:** Decisive and cheap, no extra reads: add a log-only hook in llama.cpp build_moe_ffn (copy of the work tree, never the build dir) that computes next-layer router top-k from the current residual and prints recall@8/@12 vs the actual routing over 100 decode tokens on Qwen3-30B. Go if mean recall >= .8 beyond layer 4. Then the sim: feed the measured miss list into pf.py's overlap formula t = max(C, prefetch_bytes) + demand.
- **Prior art:** Published: Speculating Experts https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19289 (quasi-hidden-state router, ~90% recall, 1-layer lead, 9-14% TPOT on GPU+DRAM where compute is only 8-13% of TPOT); Fate cross-layer gate, ProMoE, pre-attention prediction 2511.10676 (all already listed in notes, which call a hidden-state predictor 'a stronger variant than the id-only table'). New here: the regime (CPU compute 62-70% of token, NVMe, no PCIe) where the same recall converts into 1.4-1.5x instead of 5-14%.
- **Skeptic (weakened, corrected 1.35x):** Arithmetic mostly reproduces, but the headline 1.5x is the idealised corner, and several terms are optimistic.

1. The slot count was tuned so serial time matched the measured 0.289 s (hit about 0.68, about 118-123 misses per token).
   - With no contention, recall 0.9 on misses gives 1.40x (db), 1.41x (story) and 1.44x (code). Perfect recall gives 1.45-1.50x.
   - Recall 0.7 gives 1.25-1.32x. Recall 0.5 gives 1.17-1.21x.
   - The proposal's 1.51x is 0.289 / 0.191 using a flat 10% residual. Per-layer burstiness (some layers have 6-8 misses, about 5-7 ms of reads, against a 3.75 ms window) leaves more exposed than 10%, so the bound drops to about 1.4x.
2. Recall is applied to the wrong set. The paper's ~0.90 is recall of the whole true top-k. Prefetch only pays on experts that are NOT already resident, which are the colder, less predictable ones. Our id-only data shows hot-set recall exceeds the all-expert recall (.42 vs .38 at 8, .61 vs .54 at 16). Recall on misses is probably below 0.9, and no number for it exists. Realistic range 0.6-0.8, which gives 1.25-1.35x.
3. Compute-core contention is not free. In my replay, if prefetch costs the compute side 10% (a 4.1 ms layer), the gain at recall 0.9 falls to 1.31x. At 20% (4.5 ms) it falls to 1.22x. At 33% (5.0 ms; for example 3 compute threads plus 1 I/O thread) it falls to 1.11x, and to 0.96x at recall 0.5. We measured that 8 threads on 4 cores collapse to 0.26 tok/s, so oversubscription is fragile, and ggml threads spin-wait at barriers. The 4 cores are fully used by compute, so an I/O thread plus pread copies is not free. Flash-MoE's reported net loss on Apple silicon is a warning, but I did not verify it.
4. The 0.18 s "compute" is not the compute ceiling. The 55 GFLOP/s ceiling implies about 9 tok/s, or 0.111 s, for the 30B. So about 0.07 s of the 0.289 s token is unexplained overhead (barriers, pool copy, allocation). It may not hide behind prefetch, or it may partly be I/O-induced. The bound uses 0.18 as a floor, which is fair, but the real floor is unknown.
5. Possible double counting with existing levers. combo_model.py already has an overlap lever (phi, precision 0.43) worth 1.26x on the Mac in COMPARISON.md. The new idea is the improvement over that lever's coverage, not an independent 1.5x. Stacking with pin/top-k also interferes in our own table (for example aware6+overlap 1.62 vs product 1.74).
6. 125B target: 0.314 s to 0.228 s (3.18 to 4.39 tok/s) stays under the 4.58 tok/s compute ceiling, so the ceiling is not violated. But the 1.38x there also relies on recall 0.9 and zero contention. The 8 GB case is bandwidth-saturated (4.4 ms read vs 4.5 ms compute), so it is a ceiling, not a forecast.
7. The window is shorter than a full layer. The prediction can only run after attention at layer l, so the lead is layer-l FFN plus attention of l+1, and the quasi-state input is approximate. The paper's recall comes from larger-batch GPU runs.
8. Prior art confirms the mechanism but not the gain in this regime.
   - Speculating Experts (arXiv 2603.19289) reports up to 14% TPOT, offloading to CPU RAM over PCIe on GPU.
   - PreScope (arXiv 2509.23638) is on V100/A100 with PCIe. It says prefetch and on-demand loads share bandwidth, an uncoordinated prefetch degrades to on-demand loading, and it reports no SSD measurements and no wasted-prefetch rate.
   - SP-MoE and HOBBIT say wrong prefetches can be worse than on-demand loading, and contention breaks deep prefetch.
   - A 2026 preprint (Edge0, 2609.18063) argues that one-layer-ahead reads on SSD cannot reliably hide behind compute. I did not read it in full.
   No source measures hidden-state prefetch on a CPU-compute plus NVMe box, so nothing contradicts the idea, but nothing supports 1.5x either.
9. Quality is lossless as stated. Wasted prefetches can evict useful resident experts (cache pollution), which the bound ignores.

Corrected bound: about 1.4x with ideal recall and no contention, about 1.35x as a defensible upper bound, and a realistic 1.15-1.3x. The 1.5x figure is not supported.

### policy #2: Hit-first in-layer overlap: compute resident experts while this layer's missing experts are read
- **Gap:** Not modelled in combo_model.py (its overlap term covers only predicted/prefetched traffic via phi). Measured: 34% of the 8 expert reads per layer are demand misses on the Mac (0.11 s/token) and 23% at 125B 16 GB, all currently stall the layer; resident experts are 66-77% of the expert compute that could run during that wait. Expert FFN share of compute: 8x3x2048x768x48 = 1.81 B of 3.3 B active = 0.55 (Qwen3-30B, derived from config); 0.65 assumed for the 125B (estimate).
- **Change:** Split the layer's expert matmul into a resident subset and a missing subset. After the router, issue pool reads for the missing experts, immediately compute the resident ones, then compute each missing expert as its read lands (sum is commutative, so output is bit-compatible up to float order). Needs the pool to expose a per-layer residency mask and an async read-completion wait; stacks with idea 1 (which removes most misses before the router even runs).
- **Bound:** Mac: hit compute = 0.66*0.55*0.18 = 0.065 s hidden; exposed tail ~0.01 s (last miss expert); t = 0.289-0.065+0.01 = 0.234 -> 1.23x. 125B 16 GB: hit compute 0.77*0.65*0.218 = 0.109 s >= read 0.096 s so all read hidden; t ~ 0.218+0.01 = 0.228 -> 1.38x. 125B 8 GB (hit ~.5): hides 0.5*0.65*0.218 = 0.071 s of 0.427 -> 1.2x. All estimates; plausibility ~0.55 because ggml runs mul_mat_id as one op and the 4 compute threads leave no spare core for completion handling.
- **Cheapest test:** Micro-benchmark without the model: with read_bench.py's pool reader, time one synthetic layer (3 resident + 3 missing 2.7 MB experts, 4 compute threads doing a matching Q4_K matvec) serial vs overlapped; confirms the min(hit_compute, read) saving and the thread contention. Then a patch that reorders only the compute (no overlap) to check bit-compatibility.
- **Prior art:** Published in the GPU regime: CPM-2 INFMOE reorders experts so each load is covered by computation of earlier-loaded experts (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.10715); MoEpic computes cache-hit segments early to extend the overlap window (https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/moepic). Not in our notes or combo_model.
- **Skeptic (weakened, corrected 1.25x):** Arithmetic recomputed from M23 and the pool patch. The core mechanism is sound, but the 1.38x headline does not hold, and the 125B figure is the weakest part.

1. Mac baseline (M23, 6 GB pool, 66% hit): 0.289 s/token, of which reads are 0.11 s and compute is 0.18 s. Per layer that is 2.3 ms of read and 3.75 ms of compute. Expert compute is 0.55 of that, 2.06 ms, and this is the only work that can overlap reads, because attention runs before the router. The ceiling on the saving is min(read, post-router expert compute) = 2.06 ms per layer, minus the tail. The tail is the last landing expert's compute plus the per-op barriers. I estimate about 0.5 ms per layer. A saving of roughly 1.5 ms x 48 = 0.07 s is therefore the most that is credible. The proposer's 0.065 s (hits only) lands in the same range. Contention costs about 0.01 s (DMA traffic on DRAM-bound decode plus one or two I/O threads). That gives t = 0.289 - 0.07 + 0.01 = 0.23 s, or 1.25x. The proposer's 1.23x holds; 1.25x is the cap for the Mac.

2. The 125B 1.38x is optimistic. It assumes a tail of only 0.01 s. In this pool the three tensors (gate, up, down) are separate mul_mat_id ops, each ending in a barrier. The pool's load runs after a barrier on all compute threads, so the read is synchronous. A real hit-first scheme needs the reads for all three tensors issued at the gate op, plus a per-expert completion wait. The down op's misses land last, so the exposed tail is more like 0.02-0.03 s. Redo: serial t = 0.218 + 0.096 = 0.314. Overlapped t = 0.218 + 0.025 (tail) + 0.01 (contention) = 0.253 s. Speedup = 0.314 / 0.253 = 1.24x. Using the 0.65 expert share, the post-router expert compute is 0.142 s against a 0.096 s read, so the overlap does not cap it. Realistic range is 1.22-1.30x, with 1.30x the ceiling. At 8 GB and hit 0.5 the read is 0.21 s and the saving is capped at about 0.12 s, so about 1.2x.

3. Ballast regime (1.72 tok/s, 0.58 s/token): about 0.40 s of reads against only about 0.1 s of overlappable expert compute. The saving is at most 0.1 s, so 0.58 / 0.48 = 1.2x at best. Reads dominate and overlap cannot hide them.

4. Double counting. The proposal says it stacks with idea 1 (prefetch before the router). It does not multiply. Both attack the same 0.11 s read stall, and the hideable amount is capped at the post-router expert compute. After idea 1 removes misses, idea 2's gain shrinks toward zero. Combined gain is bounded by the single overlap ceiling, not 1.38 x idea 1. M23 already noted that perfect token-level overlap would give 6-8 tok/s. That is a looser bound than this idea's, but it shows the overlap concept is already in the notes.

5. Thread budget. 8 threads collapse the speed 11x because ggml barriers spin on oversubscribed cores. Today's pool reads are done by the compute threads themselves, so blocking in pread idles those cores. Overlap needs dedicated sleeping I/O threads. M23 shows 2 threads still reach 2.74 vs 2.98 tok/s (-8%), which suggests decode is DRAM-bound and a 2+2 split is cheap. But M15 shows 1 thread gets only 1.99 GB/s and 4 in flight gets 3.11 GB/s. Two I/O threads give about 2.7 GB/s, so read time rises about 15% (2.3 to 2.65 ms), which trims the gain a little. This does not kill the idea, but it is the reason for the contention term. Rated cost is not "cheap": it needs a fused or reordered gate/up/down path with a per-expert wait, not just a micro-benchmark.

6. Quality: none (same math, reordered). Float-sum order changes, so not bit-identical.

7. Prior art read. MoEpic (https://arxiv.org/html/2509.08342v1) explicitly "overlaps the computation of cached experts with the loading of missing experts". It runs on 8x RTX A6000 over PCIe 4.0 with 96-480 expert VRAM budgets, reporting TPOT 85 ms vs 136-248 ms for Mixtral. That gain includes its segment splitting and cache configuration, so it cannot be attributed to the overlap alone. The vLLM CPU-offload RFC (https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/issues/33869) overlaps GPU compute, CPU compute and copies, but has no measured numbers and was closed as not planned. Both are GPU regimes with separate compute and copy engines. Neither contradicts the idea. Neither supports 1.38x on a 4-core CPU where compute and I/O share cores and DRAM. The idea is a variant of known technique.
  - missed prior art: 
  - decisive test: Run read_bench.py-style synthetic layers on the Mac. Each layer: 8 experts of 2.7 MB split into gate/up/down, 3 resident + 3 missing (a 2.7 MB x 3 miss read). Run this with the real mul_mat_id Q4_K matvec on 2 compute threads and 2 sleeping pread threads. Compare against a serial baseline of 4 threads reading, then a barrier, then 4 threads computing. ### policy #3: Decayed-frequency eviction plus routing-replay lookahead (layer-phase aware) in the expert pool
- **Gap:** Measured: LRU-Belady gap .22 at 2 fp, .12-.15 at 4 fp, .149 at 3.7 fp (.762 vs .898). EWMA(hl 16) closes 27% of it at 3.7 fp: .762 -> .799 (+.037), 2 fp .549 -> .581, 4 fp .793 -> .826, 6 fp .921 -> .925. Adding continuation replay (match the previous two tokens' sets to an earlier position, protect the next 16 tokens that followed it): replay+EWMA .809 at 3.7 fp, .591 at 2 fp (+.047/+.042 vs LRU; misses -20%/-9%). Perfect 16-token lookahead: .891 (misses -54%). Notes' slru/pin were 0.98-1.0x, but EWMA is a different policy.
- **Change:** Replace pool LRU with priority = decayed per-key use count (half-life ~16 tokens, lazy heap, ~40 lines), evict lowest priority not used by the current token, ties by recency; optionally protect keys appearing in the tokens that followed the best-matching earlier routing context (suffix-match over the last 2 tokens' expert sets, like prompt-lookup but on routing).
- **Bound:** Realizable (replay+EWMA, 3.7 fp): misses .238 -> .191 (-20%). Mac: read 0.11*0.8 = 0.088, t = 0.268 -> 1.08x. 125B 16 GB: 0.096*0.8 = 0.077, t = 0.295 -> 1.06x; at 2 fp (8 GB): misses -9% -> 1.04x. Ceiling with a true 16-token oracle: misses -54%: Mac 0.18+0.05 = 0.23 -> 1.26x; 125B 0.262 -> 1.20x. The ceiling needs a future-routing source that batch-1 decode does not have, so 1.06-1.08x is the honest expectation.
- **Cheapest test:** Already run on traces (combo.py, phase.py). Decisive next step: implement EWMA in the explicit pool (free list + lazy heap), run the Mac 9 GB ballast with 3 prompts x 200 tokens (warm-up must be amortised: traces here are only 250-300 tokens) against LRU, same prompts, interleaved, with a null pair. Go if tok/s >= +4%.
- **Prior art:** FlashMoE learned recency+frequency policy, +7% tok/s on Qwen3-30B over LRU (https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.17063); NeurIPS 2026 'Fewer Expert Transfers Without Changing Routing: Expert Eviction' (https://neurips.cc/virtual/2026/156996): future-demand eviction cuts transfers 2.1x, within 3.5% of optimal, 16-token lookahead recovers 96%, past-frequency rule 27% (our traces reproduce 27% and ~95%); MELINOE LFU/LRU interpolation https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.11192 ; SpecMD Least-Stale https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.03921 ; caching analysis https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.05814 (LFU variant beats LRU).

### policy #4: In-context routing retrieval: early-layer signature kNN to prefetch the rest of the token (long lead, no hidden-state math)
- **Gap:** Measured recall of the true layer-l set (l>=6): previous-token set .46@8, hot-window .42@8/.61@16, cross-layer id table .38/.54, signature kNN (layers 0-3 sets of the current token matched to past tokens, 5 neighbours voting) .62@8 / .81@16. Cache sim at 3.7 fp (lead 3 layers, B=8..16): demand hit .762 -> .828-.841, prefetch 63-182 reads/token with 43-83 useful; sim speed 1.09-1.11x vs serial LRU; at 2 fp 1.19x (B=8) and 1.24x (B=12), B=16 collapses to 0.75x from pollution; at 6 fp 1.00x. With only a cross-domain dictionary (other traces) it falls to 1.01x, i.e. the gain comes from in-context repetition (code, structured output).
- **Change:** After layer 3 of each token, hash the 4 early-layer expert sets, find the nearest earlier tokens of this session (last ~300), and prefetch the union of their layer-l sets for layers 4..47 as lead permits, bounded to the idle read budget; no model hooks beyond logging expert ids. Can be combined with idea 1 as a cheap fallback predictor.
- **Bound:** From pf.py with C=0.18 s, 0.84 ms per miss, prefetch hidden under compute: t = max(C, prefetch reads) + demand reads; 2 fp: 0.326 -> 0.263 = 1.24x; 3.7 fp: 0.257 -> 0.233 = 1.10x; 6 fp ~1.0x. Perfect-prediction ceiling at 3.7 fp is 0.257/0.18 = 1.43x, so this predictor realises ~25% of the available gap. Estimates, not measurements.
- **Cheapest test:** Extend pf.py to a leave-one-trace-out plus 'prompt-lookup-style' setting on the real prefill-trace patch output (moe-prefill-trace.patch) so the history includes the prompt; if hit-converted-to-overlap stays >= 1.08x at 3.7 fp, add the id-hash search to the pool (about 60 lines). Kill if the cross-domain case is the realistic one (1.01x).
- **Prior art:** MoE-Infinity matches the current partial Expert Activation Matrix to stored request-level EAMs and prefetches/ranks by layer proximity (https://arxiv.org/html/2401.14361v3; batch size 1, GPU+host DRAM, needs all weights in host RAM). Token-level, id-only, NVMe/CPU version is ours; ExpertFlow and ProMoE use learned predictors instead.

### policy #5: Miss-conditional skip of low-rank experts (drop only when the expert is both missing and in the bottom gate-weight slots)
- **Gap:** Measured on traces (slots are gate-weight ranked): at 3.7 fp LRU the bottom two slots carry 32.5% of all misses on db ((.283+.332)/1.895), vs 25% if misses were uniform; story and code similar (slot-7 miss .25-.39 vs slot-0 .16-.26); at 2 fp bottom-2 share ~29-30%. Existing aware6/top-6 levers drop two experts always (top-6 = +4.7% ppl, top-4 +30.9%) or are unmeasured in quality.
- **Change:** At decode, if an expert is not resident and its slot rank >= 6 (or its normalised gate weight < tau), do not read it; renormalise or keep the native denominator (the pending denominator-anchoring test). Fetch every miss in the top slots. On average 0.58 of 8 experts per layer are dropped (vs 2 for top-6), so the dropped gate mass is roughly 29% of what top-6 drops.
- **Bound:** Reads fall by 30-32%. Mac: 0.289 -> 0.18+0.11*0.68 = 0.255 -> 1.13x. 125B 16 GB: 0.314 -> 0.218+0.096*0.68 = 0.283 -> 1.11x. 125B 8 GB: 0.427 -> 0.218+0.209*0.7 = 0.364 -> 1.17x. Extending to rank>=5 (3 slots, ~46% of misses on db) gives ~1.25x at 8 GB with more quality risk. Quality estimate (not measured): linear scaling of top-6's +4.7% by dropped mass gives ~+1.4% ppl.
- **Cheapest test:** Perplexity run on Qwen3-30B (wikitext, 2k tokens) with a log-only hook that simulates an LRU pool of 3.7 fp and zeroes (and renormalises) the weights of missing experts in slots 6-7; compare with expert_used_count 8 and 6 that the handoff already ran. Go if ppl loss <= 1.5% and the measured drop rate matches 0.58/layer.
- **Prior art:** A Research Square preprint (rs-11268552, Qwen3-Next-80B-A3B, abstract only seen in a search snippet, unverified) reports that fetching only first-choice or weight>=0.15 misses costs +0.35% ppl vs +5.7% for substituting every miss, and 2.5-3.0x faster per token at +0.7-1.5% ppl. Related: HOBBIT low-precision replacement of unimportant misses https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.01433 ; expert substitution (Zhu et al. 2025) cited in SpecMD https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.03921 ; cache-conditional routing P-0055. What is new for us: the rank-ordered miss profile measured on our traces and the explicit link to the unmeasured aware6 lever in combo tables.

### bytes #1: Make the dense weights resident (mlock or pool-load attention, router, head) so the pool path stops page-faulting through mmap
- **Gap:** M22 (vault, Measurements): with the pool the Mac at 9 GB ballast still pages in 0.06-0.07 GiB per token through mmap, for 1.72 tok/s (0.58 s/token). Expert reads are already inside the pool, so the leftover mmap traffic is plausibly the non-expert tensors (about 0.76 GB read per token, from the header parse). Those are never pinned. Plain mmap paging runs at 0.28-0.60 GB/s (M04/M15). The faults are not attributed to a tensor class anywhere in the notes, so this is the one real caveat.
- **Change:** After load, mlock (or copy into anonymous wired memory, or read once via pread into the pool's arena) every non-*_exps tensor, about 1.0 GB for the 30B (attention 0.5 GB, head 0.26 GB, router 0.05 GB, embedding rows stay lazy) and the stated 1.8-2.1 GB for the 125B. Hold it outside the evictable page cache so the pool and ballast cannot push it out.
- **Bound:** Ballast regime: 0.065 GiB = 70 MB per token at the ~0.34 GB/s mmap rate = 0.205 s of the 0.58 s token. Removing it gives 0.58 / 0.375 = 1.55x, shaded to 1.45x because some of the 70 MB may be pool metadata or embedding rows. At a normal 16 GB fit with no ballast the fault traffic may be near zero, so the gain there is 1.0x. Treat 1.45x as the ceiling for the memory-pressure regime only. Plausibility is mid, because the fault source is unverified.
- **Cheapest test:** Rerun the M22 pool arm (8 tokens, 1500 MB pool, 9 GB ballast) with vm_stat pageins sampled per token (or a mincore pass over the non-expert tensor ranges after the run), once as-is and once with --mlock restricted to non-expert tensors. Kill if tok/s gain is below 5% or pageins do not fall. About 10 minutes, no new code beyond a tensor-name filter.
- **Prior art:** Known practice: llama.cpp --mlock locks the whole file (https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp, common docs), and our own F20 mlocks a hot expert set. Selective mlock of only the dense tensors while experts stream is not documented in our notes or in a quick search. Regime: memory pressure on small-RAM machines.
- **Skeptic (weakened, corrected 1.20x):** Sources: M22 and M23 (notes/Measurements); dev.to "Running a 26B MoE on an 8 GB Jetson by streaming experts from SSD" (https://dev.to/anyesh/running-a-26b-moe-on-an-8-gb-jetson-by-streaming-experts-from-ssd-2jpf); the llama.cpp --mlock docs. I read the search summaries only, not the full pages, and I did not fetch the viblo.asia guide, so the prior-art check is thin.

1. The fault source is unattributed, and the most likely cause is a one-off cold start, not steady-state faulting. M22 ran 8 tokens with a cold process. The dense tensors total about 1.0 GB on the 30B. Touching them once costs 1.0 GB / 8 tokens = about 0.125 GiB per token if the whole set faults in. The observed 0.06-0.07 GiB per token x 8 = about 0.5 GiB is about half of that, which fits a first touch of the dense tensors. Dense tensors are read every token, so under LRU they should stay resident after first touch. The page cache has about 7 GB free, minus about 1.5 GB for the pool, minus the OS. So steady-state faults should be near zero. mlock would then only move the 1 GB cold load to startup, which costs 0.3 to 3 s once and is not removed. The steady-state gain would be about 1.0x. Part of the gain is a benchmark artifact of the short 8-token run.

2. The 0.34 GB/s rate may not apply. The 0.28-0.60 GB/s figure comes from plain mmap paging. Faults on dense tensors that follow the pool path may be 16 KB random faults. Those can be slower than 0.34 GB/s, so the 0.205 s estimate is uncertain in both directions.

3. Part of the 0.58 s token is unexplained, which weakly supports the idea. From M23, reads at 3000 MB take about 415 MiB per token, about 0.13 s at 3.1 GB/s. With a smaller 1500 MB pool, hit rate is lower, so reads are maybe 0.15 to 0.2 s. Compute is about 0.11 s (55 GFLOP/s ceiling, about 9 tok/s), and reads and compute are serial. That sums to about 0.26 to 0.31 s against 0.58 s measured. The residual of 0.27 to 0.32 s could be pageins, a cold pool, or pool sync overhead. Cold-pool misses are a rival explanation, because the pool starts empty in 8-token runs. Dense pageins are not the only candidate. If the pageins are real and steady, the maximum gain is 0.58 / (0.58 - 0.2) = 1.5x.

4. The gain is limited to the memory-pressure regime. At a normal 16 GB fit with no ballast, M23 shows 3-3.5 tok/s with the pool, and the faults are negligible. The pinned 1 to 2 GB comes out of the pool (about 3 hit points per GB near the knee). On a 16 GB machine running the 125B model, the dense set is about 2 GB, and it is hot every token, so the kernel already keeps it resident. The 125B target is compute-capped at 4.58 tok/s, and the pool path is already near that cap in the unpressured case.

5. Prior art. llama.cpp --mlock is whole-model only and has no per-tensor option (per the search summary). The dev.to Jetson writeup describes a fork that keeps attention, router and shared-expert weights resident while the routed experts stream from SSD. That is essentially this idea, so it is published, not novel, and it reports bitwise-identical output. The regime there is an 8 GB device.

Corrected ceiling: 1.2x in the 8-token ballast benchmark, where cold faults are partly an artifact. About 1.0x in steady state and in the unpressured regime. The proposed 1.45 is reachable only if the faults are steady-state, which the numbers argue against. Quality risk is none.
  - missed prior art: 
  - decisive test: Rerun the M22 pool arm at 9 GB ballast with two changes. First, run 32 tokens, or discard tokens 1-8 as warm-up. Second, sample vm_stat pageins per token, or run mincore over the non-expert tensor ranges. If the pageins per token fall to near 0 after warm-up, the idea is refuted and the gain is only a startup cost. If pageins stay at 0.06 GiB or more per token after warm-up, apply a non-expert-only mlock, and keep the idea only if tok/s rises by 5% or more.

### bytes #2: Quantised KV cache and a KV-aware expert budget: treat context memory as cache capacity lost
- **Gap:** Qwen3-30B KV is 98,304 B per token (48 layers x 2 x 4 KV heads x 128 x 2 B, f16): 0.40 GB at 4k, 1.6 GB at 16k, 3.2 GB at 32k. Every decode token reads all of it in attention. None of the notes' traces or sweeps include context length; the pool tests used short prompts. The replayed hit curve goes 0.63 to 0.79 from 1000 to 1500 entries (1.4 GB), so freed bytes translate directly to hits.
- **Change:** Run K and V as q8_0 (or K q8_0 / V q4_0), and let the pool size be set after the KV allocation (pool = RAM - dense - KV(ctx) - OS). Optionally add a sliding or sink window for very long contexts.
- **Bound:** Long-context case, 16k: f16 KV read per token 1.6 GB at an assumed 20 GB/s DRAM = 0.08 s, q8_0 halves it (-0.04 s); freed 0.8 GB = about 280 entries = about +0.09 hit, so miss I/O goes about 26% down, 0.11 to 0.08 s (-0.03 s, Mac numbers). Against a 0.29 + 0.08 = 0.37 s token that is 0.37 / 0.30 = 1.23x. At 4k: freed 0.2 GB, +2 hit points, attention read 0.02 s: about 1.02x. The ceiling is regime-bound and applies only to long prompts or chats.
- **Cheapest test:** Memory arithmetic plus the existing simulator: rerun ram_sweep with pool = RAM - dense - KV(ctx) for ctx 2k/8k/16k at f16 vs q8_0, no model run needed. Then one llama-bench decode at context 8k and 16k with -ctk/-ctv q8_0 vs f16 (the DRAM-read term only).
- **Prior art:** llama.cpp already exposes -ctk/-ctv q8_0/q4_0; KV quantisation papers such as KIVI (arXiv 2402.02750) cover the quality side. The coupling of KV size to expert-cache capacity on a disk-streaming MoE is not discussed in the notes or in the search results.
- **Skeptic (weakened, corrected 1.06x):** Verdict: weakened. The memory arithmetic checks out, but the decode-time arithmetic does not. I could not read the repo's hit-curve data, and my web search found only secondary sources.

What holds:
- KV per token is 48 x 2 x 4 x 128 x 2 B = 98,304 B, so 1.6 GB at 16k.
- q8_0 stores 34 bytes per 32 values, 0.53x of f16. That frees about 0.75 GB at 16k, about 260 experts at ~2.85 MB each (18.6 GB / 6144).

Why the 1.25x bound fails:
1. The attention term is compute-bound at 16k, not DRAM-bound.
   - Decode attention FLOPs per token are 4 x ctx x 128 x 32 query heads x 48 layers, about 0.79 MFLOP per context token.
   - At 16k that is 12.9 GFLOP. At our 55 GFLOP/s ceiling it takes about 0.23 s, against the proposal's 0.08 s memory-read estimate.
   - Halving the bytes does not shorten a compute-bound step. q8_0 attention on CPU also adds a dequantisation cost.
   - A CPU-only report of q8_0 KV (single, low-spec test; sources below) shows 1-2% slower generation and 11-19% slower prompt processing.
   - Quantised V in llama.cpp requires flash attention, so the CPU flash-attention kernel's speed becomes the deciding term.
   - The claimed -0.04 s is therefore about 0, and could be negative.
2. The hit gain is probably overstated.
   - The quoted slope (0.63 to 0.79 over 1000 to 1500 entries) is from the low, steep part of the curve.
   - The measured pool run was already about 2100 entries (6 GB) at 66% hit.
   - The two numbers are inconsistent, so the marginal slope at the operating point is flatter. LRU curves are concave, so +0.09 hit for 280 entries is an upper end.
   - Taking the full +0.09 as generous, miss I/O goes 0.11 s to 0.081 s, a saving of 0.03 s.
3. Corrected token time at 16k.
   - Baseline: 0.29 + 0.23 attention = 0.52 s (this assumes the 0.29 s figure excludes long-context attention, which I could not verify).
   - Optimised: 0.52 - 0.03 = 0.49 s.
   - Speedup: 0.52 / 0.49 = 1.06x.
   - At 4k the freed 0.2 GB gives about +2 hit points, about 0.006 s, so about 1.01x.
4. Regime.
   - Prefilling 16k tokens on this machine is itself minutes long, so long context is a niche regime for it.
   - KV size for the 125B target is unknown. A hybrid or GQA-heavy model could have a much smaller KV, which shrinks the effect further.
   - The 4.58 tok/s compute ceiling for the 125B only makes the I/O share smaller.
5. Quality.
   - q8_0 is near-lossless in the sources I read.
   - Sources disagree on whether q4_0 hurts K or V more. One reports roughly 0.4% perplexity for q4 K and 1.4% for q4 V; the figures are model-dependent.
   - The 4.7% penalty for top-6 on Qwen3-30B is a separate cost; do not mix it in.
6. Prior art: llama.cpp -ctk/-ctv and KIVI are known. Nothing I found studies KV bytes against the expert-cache budget, so the coupling is only trivially novel; it is plain memory accounting.

### bytes #3: Gate-rank mixed precision on misses: read low-weight experts at 2-3 bit, keep top slots at full precision
- **Gap:** Trace replay (angle_bytes/b.py, LRU at 1000-1500 entries): slots 5-8 of the 8 hold 57-59% of misses (slot 8 alone 15.5-18%). Slot order is assumed to be router-weight order, which is unconfirmed. Per-token I/O is 0.096 s (125B, 16 GB) or about 0.11 s of 0.29 s (Mac, M23). COMPARISON.md D already prices all-experts 3-bit at only 1.02-1.04x at 16-32 GB, and cache-aware 6-of-8 at 1.13-1.16x.
- **Change:** Keep a second, smaller copy of each expert in a Q2_K-class format on disk (about 0.58x the bytes of Q4_K, 2.625 vs 4.5 bpw). On a miss, read the full-precision copy for slots 1-2 and the low-precision copy for slots 3-8 (HOBBIT thresholds on cumulative gate mass). Cache entries carry their precision and an LHU-style upgrade rule.
- **Bound:** Slots 3-8 carry about 78% of misses (1 - 0.115 - 0.115 = 0.77). Miss bytes become 0.23 + 0.77 x 0.58 = 0.68 of before, so I/O drops 0.096 x 0.32 = 0.031 s. Time goes 0.314 to 0.283 s, which is 1.11x serial. If only slots 5-8 go low-precision: 0.43 + 0.57 x 0.58 = 0.76, saving 0.023 s and giving 1.08x. On the Mac proxy: 0.11 x 0.32 = 0.035 s of 0.29 s gives 1.14x. Gains shrink under overlap and at a higher hit rate (16-32 GB: 1.03-1.05x).
- **Cheapest test:** Two parts. (1) Offline: dump the router weight with each trace line (a one-line change in the existing trace patch) and compute the cumulative-mass distribution by slot, plus the share of misses with cumulative mass above 0.6. Seconds of analysis once re-traced. (2) Quality: one perplexity run with slots 3-8 replaced by Q2_K or Q3_K experts on Qwen3-30B, compared with top-6 (+4.7% measured). Kill if ppl rises more than 3%.
- **Prior art:** HOBBIT (Tang et al., arXiv 2411.01433, read in full): thresholds T1=0.6 and T2=0.9 on cumulative gate mass gave 67% high / 30% low / 3% skipped on Mixtral-8x7B top-2. The paper reports task-score loss at or below 1% for Float16+Int4 and Int8+Int2. Mixed-precision loading alone gave 1.19-1.57x end to end, on GPU+SSD. Our regime (CPU, top-8 of 128, tail slots with smaller weights) is untested. No perplexity is reported for the tail loads.

### bytes #4: Block-aligned intra-expert sparsity: permute intermediate neurons so whole 256-wide down blocks can be skipped
- **Gap:** The per-token expert read is 1.30 GB (125B) or about 0.37 GB cold (30B) and every byte of an expert is touched. Qwen3-30B's expert FFN width is 768 (3 Q4_K blocks per down row). The down tensor is 1/3 of expert bytes and FLOPs. Sub-expert sparsity appears nowhere in the notes (grep: no gate-first or partial-row entry).
- **Change:** Offline: cluster each expert's intermediate neurons by co-activation on calibration text and permute them. Gate and up rows are independent rows, so their permutation is lossless; down columns need a requantisation along the permuted axis, with small noise. At run time read gate+up (2/3 of the bytes), compute silu(g)*u, then read and multiply only the 256-wide down blocks whose activation energy is above a threshold (second small read; first read must stay above 1 MB to keep the NVMe rate).
- **Bound:** With 3 down blocks per row and one skipped on average: bytes -1/9 of the expert and FLOPs -1/9 of the expert share. 125B: I/O 0.096 x 0.11 = 0.011 s; expert compute about 58% of 0.218 s = 0.126 s x 0.11 = 0.014 s; total 0.025 / 0.314 = 1.08x. If the 125B has wider experts and half of the down blocks can be skipped: 1/6 each, 0.016 + 0.021 = 0.037 s, 1.13x. I use 1.10x as the ceiling, before the cost of the second, smaller read (extra latency, about one 0.5 MB read per miss).
- **Cheapest test:** Python-free analysis on the activations only: needs one forward pass dump, which we may not run in this task. Planned test: dump post-SwiGLU activations for 200 tokens on the 30B, report the fraction of 256-blocks with energy under 5% of the expert total, before and after permutation by co-activation. Kill if under 25% of blocks are skippable.
- **Prior art:** TEAL (arXiv 2408.14690, read): training-free 40-50% activation sparsity, 1.53-1.8x decode on dense Llama/Mistral, nothing on MoE experts. SmallThinker (arXiv 2507.20984): ReLU-trained experts show above 0.6 neuron sparsity in deeper layers, with SSD offload. PowerInfer-2 clusters neurons for flash reads on phones (ReLU models). Block-aligned permutation of a SwiGLU MoE with Q4_K blocks is not in what I found.

### bytes #5: Remix the Q4_K_M down projections: Q6_K to Q5_K or Q4_K on streamed experts (stream-optimised quant recipe)
- **Gap:** Header parse: 24 of 48 layers store ffn_down_exps in Q6_K (1.29 MB vs 0.885 MB per expert). The result is a 3.06 MB vs 2.655 MB expert (+15%). Q4_K_M's own mix averages 2.86 MB per expert, so 5.7 GB of the 18.6 GB file is Q6_K down. Q6_K is the non-repacked, non-VNNI AVX2 vec_dot path in the local ggml (arch/x86/quants.c), which is also what separates 55 GFLOP/s (Qwen3-30B decode) from 107 GFLOP/s (OLMoE Q4_K_M decode, M16). The cause of that 2x gap is not established.
- **Change:** Offline requantise the Q6_K down experts to Q4_K (or Q5_K for the first and last layers only), keeping everything else. This is the Q4_K_S-style recipe applied to the expert tensors. It also lets the Q4_K 8x8 repack kernels apply to those experts if the layout is stored pre-repacked on disk.
- **Bound:** Average expert 2.86 to 2.655 MB (-7.2%). Pool capacity rises 7.7%, which at the replayed slope of about 0.03 hit per 100 entries near the knee adds roughly +2.5 hit points, so miss traffic goes 0.34 to 0.305 and bytes per miss fall 7.2%: miss bytes x0.83. 125B base: I/O 0.096 x 0.17 = 0.016 s. Add a Q6_K to Q4_K kernel saving I estimate at 0.01 s on Q6 layers (unmeasured; Q6 about 20% of active weights, kernel perhaps 1.5x slower per weight). Total 0.026 s of 0.314 = 1.09x; use 1.08x. At 32 GB where hit is 0.93 the gain is under 1.02x.
- **Cheapest test:** Compare llama-bench decode tok/s on the same fully-resident Qwen3-30B file requantised with --allow-requantize to uniform Q4_K for the down experts against stock, to isolate the kernel part (no disk involved). Then one perplexity run for the quality cost. Kill if the kernel part is under 3% and ppl rises more than 1.5%.
- **Prior art:** llama.cpp k-quants (Q4_K_S vs Q4_K_M use_more_bits mix) and per-tensor type overrides exist. Published k-quant tables put the Q4_K_S vs Q4_K_M perplexity gap on dense models at roughly 1% (my recollection, not checked here). Applying the choice to streamed experts and counting cache capacity is not discussed in the sources I found.

### bytes #6: Certified or screened output head: full-vocab logits only for a candidate set
- **Gap:** output.weight is Q6_K, 255 MB, read in full every token. It is about 0.31B of the 3.1B active parameters (10% of the FLOPs) and 255 MB of about 2 GB read per token. The compute ceiling already counts it. A 125B-class model with a similar vocabulary would put it near 0.6B of 6B.
- **Change:** Compute cheap approximate logits for the whole vocabulary (a low-rank or Q2 copy, 20-40 MB resident), take the top-K candidates plus a margin, and run the exact Q6_K rows only for them. Falls back to the full head when the margin is too small.
- **Bound:** Head cost is about 10% of FLOPs, so about 0.11 s x 0.10 = 0.011 s of the 0.29 s Mac token; the 125B is similar at about 0.02 s of 0.314 s. Removing about 80% of it saves at most 0.009-0.016 s: 1.03-1.05x.
- **Cheapest test:** On saved final hidden states, compare the top-1 of the approximate head with the full head for 1,000 positions and report the miss rate at the chosen margin. Kill if the screen misses the true argmax on more than 0.5% of tokens or the fallback rate is above 20%.
- **Prior art:** VocabTrim (arXiv 2506.22694) and SlimSpec (arXiv 2605.10453) trim or low-rank the draft head in speculative decoding. NVIDIA TensorRT-Edge-LLM has a vocabulary-reduction option. A certified exact-top-1 screen for the main model's head was not found in the search.

### bytes #7: Entropy-coded or entropy-aware expert pages (lossless and lossy-coded), decoded at pool fill
- **Gap:** Measured here on real Q4_K slices: nibble entropy 3.86 of 4 bits, zlib and lzma ratio 0.979, i.e. 2.1% smaller; Q6_K down slices 0.94 (zlib) / 0.92 (lzma), 6-10% smaller. Averaged over the Q4_K_M mix this is about 3.5%. Per-block min/max scaling flattens the nibble distribution, which is why it is small. Notes already had 1.02-1.03x.
- **Change:** (a) Lossless rANS on nibble and scale streams, decoded on the I/O thread at fill. (b) Lossy co-design: requantise experts to a tensor-level or entropy-penalised 4-bit code so the entropy drops to about 3 bits, then rANS-code. Decode happens once per miss and the cache holds decoded pages.
- **Bound:** (a) 0.965 x bytes saves 0.096 x 0.035 = 0.003 s of 0.314 s: 1.01x before decode cost. (b) If a 4.5 to 3.0 bpw coded page were quality-neutral, I/O drops 0.096 x 0.33 = 0.032 s. The decoder must produce the 0.3 GB of missed pages each token. At an assumed 1.5-3 GB/s per core on 4 busy cores that costs 0.025-0.05 s of CPU that compute already uses (8 threads collapse to 0.26 tok/s, so there are no spare cores). Net is about 0.0 to +0.01 s: 1.00-1.04x.
- **Cheapest test:** Already done for (a): entropy and compressor numbers above. For (b) measure single-core rANS decode GB/s on Q4_K-sized buffers on the Mac. Kill if below 4 GB/s per core.
- **Prior art:** EntroLLM (arXiv 2505.02380, read): Huffman on tensor-level quantised weights gives 1.39 effective bits at 4-bit and 5.58 at 8-bit, token generation 1.32-2.5x on a Jetson with a one-off parallel decode; the paper notes that block-based schemes (blocks of 32) flatten the distribution and hurt compressibility, which matches our 3.86 / 4. EntQuant (arXiv 2601.22787) optimises values for low entropy plus a GPU ANS decoder. Regime: GPU or bandwidth-bound edge boards, not a compute-bound CPU.

### bytes #8: Inter-expert dedup, shared base plus low-bit delta (tested and refuted)
- **Gap:** Hypothesis: experts of a layer share structure, so a resident base plus a 2-3 bit delta could replace 4.5 bpw reads. Test here: 9 experts of one layer (blk.3 gate, 76,800 sampled weights, dequantised Q4_K), mean pairwise cosine 0.003 (max 0.0115). After the best scaled mean of the other experts, 99.98-100% of an expert's energy remains.
- **Change:** Store a per-layer base expert resident and read only quantised deltas on a miss.
- **Bound:** Delta energy is the full weight energy (ratio 1.00), so a delta needs the same bits as the weight itself: bytes read x1.0 and the speedup is 1.0x. Even a perfect 2-bit delta of an uncorrelated matrix would give the same error as quantising the weights directly.
- **Cheapest test:** Done (dq.py). A second layer and the up/down tensors would confirm, but the correlation is at noise level. Do not pursue.
- **Prior art:** MoE delta/base compression papers (for example D2-MoE and MoBE, lossy low-rank, on other models) report quality loss; they find shared structure only in some models. Not re-read here.


## Angle: pipeline

Reads and compute are fully serial in the pool patch: per MoE op, thread 0 decides hits and misses, a barrier follows, all four compute threads pread the missing experts, a second barrier follows, then the matmul runs. That happens for gate, up and down separately, 3 ops x 48 layers. I replayed the real 128-expert traces through an LRU pool and a layer-level schedule model. Layer split is an estimate: 1.1-1.4 ms attention/router and 1.1-1.4 ms experts per layer, from the cached rate of 8.3 tok/s = 120 ms per token. At 66% hit the pool-size sweep reports about 353 MiB read per token, but that was a 16-token run that includes the cold fill. Steady-state LRU miss rates from the traces are lower. A pool of 2222 expert triples (about 6 GB) misses 0.26-0.77 experts per layer. A pool of 1150 (about 3 GB) misses 1.97-2.71 per layer, mean 2.36. A pool of 770 misses 3.25-3.69 per layer. I use the 1150 case as the main regime. It is close to the 125B at 16 GB, where a 0.77 hit rate means 2.3 misses per layer of 10.

Modelled serial path at that regime: about 246 ms per token, 4.1 tok/s (S0 in the table). The read share is about 126 ms and the compute floor is 120 ms.

Modelled speedups over S0 at 1150 entries:
- Async I/O with hit-first compute and sibling-tensor reads queued together: 1.27-1.33x, bit-identical output.
- Plus next-layer prediction with an assumed 80% recall of misses and 50% wasted bytes: 1.41-1.54x. The recall is an assumption, not a measurement.
- Deferring the misses' contribution by one layer: 2.05x, equal to the compute floor, with quality unmeasured.

Microbenchmarks I ran on this Mac with a 4 GB scratch file, F_NOCACHE preads, random extents:
- Two non-pool I/O threads alongside 4 spinning compute threads reach 2.65 GB/s with 0.9 MB reads, against 2.69 GB/s on an idle machine (M15). So a separate I/O helper costs almost nothing.
- Fresh posix_memalign per read vs a reused buffer: 2.83 vs 2.85 GB/s with 4 threads and 1.48 vs 1.14 with 1 thread (the single-thread gap is run noise). My guess that allocation churn explains the unexplained non-read time is refuted; the pool's own bookkeeping is not the problem.
- Queue depth: at 0.9 MB, QD1 gives 1.14-1.48 GB/s, QD2 2.33-2.65 and QD3-8 2.6-2.85. At 2.7 MB, QD1 gives 2.14 GB/s and QD3 2.81. Today's per-op queue depth (about 2.4 misses per op) is therefore already near saturation, and batching reads only helps slightly.

Co-occurrence prediction from the previous layer's experts is weak on the real traces. With a pool of 1150 triples, top-4 uncached candidates recover only 36-44% of true misses at 19-26% precision, which means about 2.8-4.1 wasted reads per useful one. That matches the 1.00-1.075x in M14. Any useful lookahead has to use hidden-state-based gating, which I could not measure without running the model.

### pipeline #1: Defer the missed experts' contribution by one layer (disk-backed expert deferral)
- **Gap:** The serial model puts about 126 ms of 246 ms per token into waiting for reads that the layer's own output needs immediately. Router output is needed before reads can start, and nothing can be hidden inside the layer beyond the e window.
- **Change:** Treat cache misses like KTransformers treats its deferred experts. Hit experts are computed and added to the residual now. For missed experts, issue the read, finish layer L without them, and add their (late) output into the residual of layer L+1, after L+1's attention and before L+1's router. Reads then overlap with the whole of L+1's attention and hit-expert compute, with no prediction needed. Optionally defer only misses with low router weight and defer at most d per layer.
- **Bound:** Token time becomes max(compute floor, read time). Compute floor is 120 ms (M10). Read time at 1150 entries is 2.36*2.7MB*48/2.7 GB/s = 113 ms, so 246/120 = 2.05x. At 770 entries reads are 169 ms, so 296/169 = 1.75x. At 2222 entries 156/120 = 1.30x. This is an upper bound: it assumes all misses can be deferred and perfect overlap.
- **Cheapest test:** Quality-only test with the whole model in RAM, no disk: in build_moe_ffn, hold back d=1,2,3 of the 8 selected experts (lowest router weight) and add their output into the next layer's residual. Measure wikitext perplexity and a few generations against the standard-renormalisation baseline. Passes if the loss is within about +1-2% at d<=2; fails near the +4.7% that top-6 costs. Then measure real speed with the pool.
- **Prior art:** KTransformers Expert Deferral (SOSP 2025): deferred experts run concurrently with the next layer's attention; reported up to 1.45x additional throughput and under 0.5% average accuracy drop, but for a CPU/GPU split on DeepSeek-V3-class models, with details such as how many experts and where merged not confirmed by me. https://lmsys.org/blog/2025-10-22-KTransformers and https://madsys.cs.tsinghua.edu.cn/publication/ktransformers-unleashing-the-full-potential-of-cpu/gpu-hybrid-inference-for-moe-models/ . I did not find it applied to disk-resident experts.
- **Skeptic (weakened, corrected 1.50x):** ARITHMETIC (consistent as a global bound). M10: 8.3 tok/s cached means a 120 ms compute floor. Read time is 2.36 misses x 2.7 MB x 48 / 2.7 GB/s = 113 ms, so 246/120 = 2.05x. This matches M23's own estimate of 6-8 tok/s for perfect overlap against 3.46 measured (0.29 s per token at 66% hit, about 0.11 s of it reads), i.e. 1.7-2.3x. The 2.05x is a perfect-overlap ceiling, not a schedule.

PROBLEM 1, MERGE POINT SHRINKS THE WINDOW. The idea merges the deferred output after L+1's attention and before L+1's router. The hiding window per layer is then only the rest of layer L (the hit experts) plus L+1's attention. Per layer, compute is 120/48 = 2.5 ms and reads are 113/48 = 2.36 ms. Attention is about 0.9B of the 3.3B active params (my estimate: 48 x about 19M params, from 2048x4096 q/o and 2048x512 k/v), roughly 28%, or about 0.7 ms. Hit experts at 66-70% hit are about 0.6-0.8 ms. So the window is about 1.3-1.5 ms against 2.36 ms of reads. The stall left is about 0.9-1.1 ms x 48 = 43-53 ms, giving a token of about 165-170 ms and 246/168 = 1.46x. This is the same one-layer-window limit that held cross-layer prefetch to a lossless 1.00-1.075x. The proposed bound ignores it.

PROBLEM 2, MISS COUNT IS NOT AVERAGE. 2.36 is a mean. Many layers miss 4-6 experts, and others miss 0. A cap of d per layer leaves the excess as stalls. Reads for layer L+1's own misses also queue behind the deferred reads, so the pipeline is bounded by max(compute, read) only if the queue never builds. Bursts break that.

PROBLEM 3, CORES SHARED. Reads are pread copies run on the same 4 physical cores as compute. M23 showed 8 threads collapse to 0.26 tok/s because of barrier spinning, and 4 threads is best. Overlapping reads with compute therefore costs compute throughput, and the floor is above 120 ms. I have no measurement of that cost.

PROBLEM 4, QUALITY RISK HIGHER THAN IN THE CITED PAPER. I read the KTransformers SOSP25 text. It defers a miss to layer k+1 and merges it at layer k+1's MoE output (O_k = I_k + S_k + R_def_{k-1} + R_imm_k). That gives a window of a whole layer including expert compute. The idea's earlier merge, before the router, loses that.

The paper's other settings:
- It keeps at least 2 immediate experts and does not defer in the last layer.
- Decode only.
- Its models have shared experts: DS-3 (256 experts, top-8) defers 6 and Qwen2-57B (64 experts, top-8) defers 4. The shared expert carries the main signal. Qwen3-30B-A3B has no shared expert, so the reported under-0.5% drop does not transfer.
- It is trained-model robustness with no fine-tuning, and the loss grows with the number deferred. Its Fig 13 shows skipping 6 costs 13.3% and deferring 6 costs 0.5% on DS-3.
- The 1.45x is CPU/GPU overlap on DeepSeek-V3-class models, from raising CPU utilisation from 74% to nearly 100%. It is a different regime from disk reads.

So the average of 2.4 deferred is within the paper's range, but quality on Qwen3-30B and on the 125B target is unmeasured. Expect more loss in early layers. If the loss exceeds about 2%, the idea is dead regardless of speed.

PRIOR ART. KTransformers Expert Deferral (SOSP25, https://madsys.cs.tsinghua.edu.cn/publication/ktransformers-unleashing-the-full-potential-of-cpu/gpu-hybrid-inference-for-moe-models/SOSP25-chen.pdf) covers the same mechanism for CPU/GPU overlap. A web search for disk-resident use found nothing, which is not proof of absence. The disk variant remains a plausible variant, not a published result.

CORRECTED BOUND. The 2.05x ceiling holds only with a full-layer window (KTransformers-style merge) and free cores. With the idea's pre-router merge, I estimate 1.4-1.5x. A realistic expectation is 1.2-1.4x, less once I/O threads contend with compute. That is an estimate, not a measurement. At the 125B target the compute ceiling is 4.58 tok/s, so the gain applies only to the stall portion there too.

  - decisive test: Two cheap tests, with no model download.

(1) Quality, whole model in RAM, no disk: implement deferral in build_moe_ffn on Qwen3-30B-A3B for d = 1, 2, 3 and 4 lowest-weight experts. Run both merge points: before L+1's router (the idea) and at the end of L+1's MoE (the KTransformers form). Measure wikitext perplexity. ### pipeline #2: Software-pipeline two independent streams (interleaved layers) so one stream's reads hide behind the other's compute
- **Gap:** M14: batching unrelated sequences amortises few loads (1.07x at N=2), so notes treated multi-stream as useless. They did not model it as a latency-hiding pipeline. Serial: 246 ms per token per stream, with read 113 ms and compute 120 ms.
- **Change:** For agent-style workloads with two or more concurrent sessions (or two speculative branches), run the layers of stream A and B offset by half a layer, so A's expert reads overlap B's attention and expert compute. No prediction, no quality risk, but each stream shares the cache and its own per-token latency does not improve.
- **Bound:** Aggregate throughput bound is 1/max(compute, reads) per token: 2 tokens per max(2*120, 2*113 ms) = 240 ms versus 2*246 = 492 ms serial, so 2.05x aggregate. Shared cache halves each stream's pool: at 770 entries per stream misses rise from 2.36 to 3.52 per layer, reads become 169 ms per token, and aggregate is 2 per max(240, 338) = 338 ms vs 592 ms serial, so 1.75x. Take 1.9x as an optimistic midpoint. Per-stream tok/s does not improve and drops by up to half.
- **Cheapest test:** Two llama-bench processes sharing the pool is not possible (pool is per process); test by replaying two traces against one LRU with the same schedule model in sched.py, interleaved, and compare aggregate time with 1150 and 2x1150 entries.
- **Prior art:** Multi-batch pipelining for offloaded inference: MoE-Lightning, Klotski and similar GPU systems (cited from memory, not read or linked); they target throughput batching on GPUs, not CPU-only single-user disk-backed decoding.
- **Skeptic (weakened, corrected 1.45x):** Aggregate throughput of two streams only, never per-stream or single-user (1.0x there). I could not confirm the 246/113/120 ms split or the 2.36 to 3.52 miss figures in M14 (it only has the 1.07x/1.13x batching lines), so they are unverified.

1. Ideal overlap on the proxy. Per token, 120 ms compute + 113 ms read, plus about 13 ms other (the 246 total is not recomputed from M14). Perfect overlap gives 246 / max(120+13, 113) = 1.85x at best, not 2.05x. The 2.05x figure drops the 13 ms of non-overlappable work.

2. Shared cache. A fair baseline is two sessions time-sliced over the same pool, not each with a full pool. M12 says the hot set transfers poorly across prompts and M14 says unrelated streams share few experts. So the proposal's own halved-pool numbers apply: reads rise to 169 ms per token. Overlapped time is then max(133, 169) = 169 ms per token. Against the shared-cache serial baseline of 133 + 169 = 302 ms, that is 1.79x. Against the full-cache serial 246 ms it is only 1.46x. The proposal mixes the two baselines, and the 1.75x mid-case is an estimate that gives the overlap full credit for a bandwidth-bound regime.

3. Resource contention that the bound ignores. Our measurements show 4 threads are best and 8 threads collapse to 0.26 tok/s. Reads use CPU for copying and page-cache work, and compute already saturates the 4 cores and DRAM bandwidth. Read and compute overlap on the same 4 cores and the same memory bus is therefore not free. I estimate 10-25% lost, which puts the realistic proxy gain around 1.3-1.45x. That figure is an estimate and has not been measured.

4. Target model. For the 125B model, compute is capped at 4.58 tok/s, about 218 ms per token. At a hit rate of 0.77, misses are about 48 layers x 10 experts x 0.23 = 110, at roughly 2.8 MB each (69 GB / (48 x 512)). That is about 309 MB, or 0.1 s at 3.1 GB/s. Compute therefore dominates, and the gain is bounded by (218 + 100) / 218 = 1.46x. With a shared 16 GB cache the hit rate falls and reads grow, so the bound is about 1.3-1.45x. A second stream also costs KV cache and pool RAM.

5. Prior art contradicts the optimism. Klotski (arXiv 2502.06888, https://arxiv.org/html/2502.06888v1) does exactly multi-batch pipelining. Its own text says more batches activate more experts and cause more I/O bubbles. It needs prefetching of hot experts to work, and its gain over FlexGen is only about 15% at batch 128. It ran on an RTX 3090 or H800 with 256-800 GB of host RAM, a 1 GB/s SSD and batches of 4-64 sequences. That is a different regime: GPU, large caches, and dense batches. Search results also described CPM-2 (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.10715), which overlaps loads with compute on separate CUDA streams, and MoE-Lightning (https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.11217), which I did not open. The idea is a variant of known work, but I found no CPU-only, disk-backed, 2-stream result.

6. Workload fit. It helps only for concurrent agent sessions. It does nothing for the single-user case that is the project's stated goal.
  - missed prior art: 
  - decisive test: Replay two different real traces (e.g. db and story) interleaved against one LRU with 1150 entries total, and again with 2x1150, using the existing schedule model in sched.py. Take per-layer miss counts for each. Compute a two-resource makespan with serial dependence (reads for layer L after routing, compute for A overlapping reads for B). Then add a measured contention factor for running 4 compute threads plus 4 read threads concurrently on the Mac. Accept the idea only if aggregate time is at least 1.3x better than time-sliced sessions sharing the same pool. Reject it otherwise.

### pipeline #3: Cache-state-aware deferral: only defer when the read would stall
- **Gap:** Of layers in the 1150-entry replay, 13-21% have zero misses and a further 24-26% have one; only about 40% have 3 or more. Deferral applied to every layer wastes quality on layers that would not stall.
- **Change:** Make deferral conditional: compute at the layer how long the pending reads will take given queue depth, and defer only the misses that cannot land within the expert window, ranked by lowest router weight and lowest layer sensitivity. A small d_max cap (1 or 2) bounds the quality loss. This combines the deferral idea with the S1 pipeline so that most layers are exact.
- **Bound:** Estimate: S1 gives 185 ms. Deferring only stalls beyond what S1 hides removes the remaining read-bound part. With d_max=2 most layers are covered (misses 0-2 in about 70% of layers at 1150 entries). Assume it removes about 70% of the 185-120=65 ms residual stall: 185-45 = 140 ms, so 246/140 = 1.75x; I round to 1.7x. Rough estimate, not simulated beyond the miss distribution.
- **Cheapest test:** Extend the offline replay with a stall predictor (reads queued at router time vs e window). Count how many experts per token would need deferring at d_max=1 and 2. If the average is below 1 expert per token-layer, run the perplexity test from the deferral idea at that d.
- **Prior art:** Not found. Related: Expert Deferral above is unconditional; cache-aware routing (our notes) changes which experts are chosen rather than when they are applied.

### pipeline #4: Next-layer router on the current hidden state, verified prefetch at low priority
- **Gap:** M14 co-occurrence prefetch from layer L-1 gets precision@8 0.51-0.55, and my miss-only replay gives 36-44% recall of true misses at 19-26% precision (2.8-4.1 wasted reads per hit); notes put lossless cross-layer gain at 1.00-1.075x. Disk utilisation is only about 38-50% of time at 2.7-3.1 GB/s, so there is bandwidth to spend on guesses.
- **Change:** At the end of layer L's attention (or when the FFN input of layer L is available), apply layer L+1's router weights (a tiny 2048x128 matmul, about 0.26 MFLOP) to the residual and take the top-8 and a few runners-up. Queue the uncached ones on the I/O helper at low priority, behind any demand read, with a cap of N extra reads per layer. The real router at L+1 then runs as usual; a wrong guess just costs a wasted read, and a miss falls back to demand loading. Output is unchanged.
- **Bound:** Model S2: predicted fraction rho of misses is read during the previous layer's window (a+e, about 2.5 ms), with waste factor w. At rho=0.8 and w=0.5 the 1150-entry regime goes from S0 246 ms to 160 ms (1.49-1.54x). At rho=0.5 and w=1.0 it is 1.41-1.46x. At 770 entries it is 1.29-1.44x; at 2222 it is 1.27-1.28x. rho is an assumed value; it is the number to measure. Ceiling with perfect prediction is S1 plus hiding the remainder, about 120 ms or 2.05x. Estimate, not a measurement.
- **Cheapest test:** Offline recall measurement with no disk effect: in the build_moe_ffn graph, also compute softmax(W_gate[L+1] @ h_L) top-8 and dump it next to the real routing, for 3 prompts of about 300 tokens. Then replay through the existing LRU simulator and score recall of true misses and waste per useful read at 1, 2 and 3 layers ahead. Go if recall of misses is at least 0.6 at waste at most 1; no-go below 0.4.
- **Prior art:** Applying the next gate to the current hidden state is published: Eliseev and Mazur, Mixtral 8 experts top-2, 1-2 experts prefetched, speculative loading raised tok/s 1.57->2.09 (T4), 2.23->2.66 (3080M), 2.05->2.28 (3060), 2.92->3.06 (A100) over LRU alone: https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.17238 (I read the HTML). HOBBIT reports 96% top-1 next-layer accuracy and about 90% at 2-3 layers ahead: https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.01433 (search snippet only). DALI adds a per-layer residual correction: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03495. Pre-gated MoE retrains the gate: https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.12066. SMoE warns that prefetching all predicted experts saturates the link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.18983. All of these were measured over PCIe, not a disk with an LRU hit rate of 0.7-0.9. Top-8 of 128 is a harder target than top-1 or top-2 of 8, and the misses are the less-predictable tail.

### pipeline #5: Async I/O helper threads plus hit-first expert compute and sibling-tensor read queueing
- **Gap:** M23: at 4 threads 0.29 s/token, of which about 0.11 s is reads (353 MiB at 3.1 GB/s) and the rest compute and sync, serial. Patch (moe-pool patch, mul_mat_id): per op, barrier, all compute threads pread, barrier, then compute; the three tensors of one expert are issued in three separate rounds. Trace replay with 1150-triple LRU: 2.36 misses per layer on average, 13% of layers have no miss, yet every miss blocks the whole 4-thread pool. Modelled serial 246 ms/token (4.1 tok/s). M10: cached compute floor 120 ms.
- **Change:** Move pread out of the ggml compute pool. 1-2 dedicated I/O threads (higher QoS, blocking in pread, not spinning) take a request list. At the gate op, as soon as the router ids are known, queue the miss reads for gate, up and down together, with each tensor's reads in expert order. Per-expert ready flags let compute threads run hit experts first, then each missed expert as it lands. Remove the two extra barriers per op. Everything is bit-identical because only timing changes.
- **Bound:** Per layer (estimate) a=1.1-1.4 ms attention/router, e=1.4-1.1 ms experts. Serial S0 = a + 3*(m*0.9MB/rate(m) + e/3). With m=2.36 that is about 5.1 ms/layer = 246 ms/token. Overlapped: a + max(R + e/8, e), R = 3*m*0.9/2.7 = 2.4 ms, giving about 3.85 ms/layer = 185 ms/token, so 1.33x with a=1.1/e=1.4 and 1.27x with a=1.4/e=1.1. At m=0.5 (6 GB pool): 1.22-1.24x. At m=3.5: 1.21-1.27x. 125B at hit 0.77 is about 1.3x by the same arithmetic (read 110 ms vs compute 218 ms per token). Only the expert-compute window e can hide reads, because the router needs the attention output, so this idea cannot reach the full 2x.
- **Cheapest test:** Standalone C microbenchmark, already run: 2 I/O threads next to 4 spinning threads give 2.65 GB/s versus 2.69 GB/s idle, so no hardware obstacle. Decisive test: patch the pool so gate-op prepare enqueues up/down reads to 2 helper threads and the matmul loop polls per-expert ready flags; run llama-bench -t 4 at the 1500 MB and 3000 MB pools and the 9 GB ballast, with the 4,225-check identity suite as the null test. Check that 4 compute threads plus 2 I/O threads do not trigger the 8-thread collapse (M23).
- **Prior art:** Layer-wise load/compute pipelining on a separate stream is standard in GPU offload (Pre-gated MoE https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.12066, Read-ME https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.19123, Eliseev and Mazur https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.17238). I found no CPU-only, disk-backed, router-ready-driven hit-first scheduling with per-expert completion flags in the results I read, but this is not an exhaustive search.

### pipeline #6: Expert-major repack of the GGUF so gate, up and down of one expert are one contiguous extent
- **Gap:** Each missed expert is currently three separate 0.9 MB reads from three tensors; M15 shows 1.25 GB/s at 1 thread and 2.69 at 4 threads for 0.9 MB, versus 1.99 and 3.11 for a contiguous 2.7 MB expert. My microbenchmark: 2.7 MB at QD1 gives 2.14 GB/s versus 1.14-1.48 for 0.9 MB, with 4 spinning threads alongside 2.00 vs 1.38. Layers with 1-2 misses are 49% of layers at 1150 entries.
- **Change:** Offline: write a second file in which each (layer, expert) stores its three weight blocks back to back, and point the pool's offsets file at it. The pool then needs one pread per miss, and a single I/O thread achieves 2.1 GB/s rather than 1.3. Lossless.
- **Bound:** With the async helper (S1), read rate rises from about 2.65 to 2.8 GB/s at QD3 (about 5%), and by up to 1.6x at QD1 (2.14 vs 1.14-1.48 GB/s). The read part of the token is about 113 ms of 185 ms S1 time, but it is partly hidden; the exposed read portion is about 65 ms. A 5-60% gain on it yields 3-30 ms, so 185/(185-12) = 1.07x on average. Estimate.
- **Cheapest test:** Read-only microbenchmark extension (already half done): compare 3 parallel 0.9 MB preads at stride vs 1 contiguous 2.7 MB pread at QD1-3, with 4 spinners. The file transform itself is a one-off script.
- **Prior art:** Bundling co-used weights into one read is the 'row-column bundling' of LLM in a flash (Apple), https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.11514 (cited from memory, not re-read), there for flash chunk size on phones.

### pipeline #7: Token-boundary read-ahead for the first layers from a token-id to expert table
- **Gap:** The sampling step and lm_head take a few ms during which the disk is idle, and the first layers of the next token have misses like any other.
- **Change:** At the end of token t the next token id is known (greedy). Look up the layer-0..2 expert sets seen previously for that token id (layer 0 routing depends mostly on the embedding) and queue them on the I/O helper while the lm_head and sampler finish.
- **Bound:** Window is lm_head and sampling, about 3-8 ms (estimate: 151k x 2048 weights at 0.56 B/weight = 0.17 GB at about 15 GB/s = 11 ms upper), against a 246 ms token: at most 8/246 = 3%, realistically 1-2%.
- **Cheapest test:** Analyse the existing traces: for repeated token ids across the three prompts, precision of the layer 0-2 expert sets given the token id. Needs token ids, which the traces do not store, so one cheap trace re-capture is required.
- **Prior art:** Not found for the CPU/disk case; the token-id routing table idea is related to token-to-expert statistics in routing-analysis papers (not verified).

Read-only OS pass on the 16 GB Mac (live state sampled with vm_stat, sysctl, pmset; no model run). Live state: 17.2 GB RAM, swap 2.1 of 3.0 GB used (encrypted), compressor holds 1.93 M pages (about 7.9 GB logical, 1.6 GB resident, 291 M compressions since boot), wired 2.9 GB, free about 6.7 GB at sample time, no thermal or performance warning (CPU_Speed_Limit 100), Spotlight index read-only on /, backupd idle, but a foreign process (another process) was burning 200% CPU, i.e. two of the four physical cores, while I looked. Main finding: the explicit pool fixed the expert path but left three OS-level leaks. (a) With the pool, 0.06-0.07 GiB per token is still paged in through mmap (M22). Those are non-expert weights and similar pages. They are faulted at the mmap rate (0.28-0.60 GB/s, M15/M21) instead of 3.1 GB/s, so they cost about 0.11-0.25 s of a 0.58 s token under ballast. (b) The pool is plain posix_memalign anonymous memory, so under pressure macOS may compress or swap its cold slots. Expert bytes are essentially incompressible, so that work is pure waste. Measured null test: zlib-1 on 80 MB sampled from a Q4_K_M MoE GGUF gives 0.977 of original size, zlib-6 gives 0.976 (script <scratch>). So an in-RAM compressed expert tier is dead (about 2% capacity gain) and the macOS compressor can only hurt expert data. (c) ggml barriers spin, and 8 threads on 4 cores collapse 11x (M23), so any neighbour process that steals a core behaves like partial oversubscription.

Prior art searches (5): llama.cpp --mlock commit (mlock disables swap and compression of model data), LLM-in-a-flash (windowing, bundling), the Postgres macOS F_RDADVISE patch (read in full: it only issues advice, reports no benchmark, and the size field is an int), llama.cpp issue 19825 (read in full: users on a 24 GB M4 see a swap death spiral with MoE on macOS and ask for a managed SSD pipeline, which our pool already is; no maintainer reply), and ggml barrier and thread-contention threads (spin-wait, oversubscription hurts, nothing macOS-QoS specific). I found no macOS NVMe benchmark of pread+F_NOCACHE against mmap, so our 3.11 vs 0.28-0.60 GB/s numbers are a primary data point.

1. Threads = physical cores (4): measured +11x versus 8 (M23). Mandatory.
2. Kill or pause foreign CPU hogs, or drop to -t 3 while one core is busy: potentially several x when contended (see idea 2); 0 when idle. Always log the load average in every A/B run (M05/M21 were noted as contended).

### os #1: Overlap pool reads with compute using kernel-blocking I/O threads (no spin) at elevated QoS
- **Gap:** M23: token 0.29 s at 66% hit, of which about 0.11 s is reads at 3.1 GB/s (353 MiB per token) done by the compute threads between barriers (patch: reads happen after a barrier, each thread takes every nth miss), so I/O and compute are serial. 8 compute threads collapse to 0.26 tok/s because ggml barriers spin.
- **Change:** Add 2 dedicated reader threads (pthread_set_qos_class_self_np user-initiated) that block in pread, so they do not spin and do not join the compute barrier; they fill the pool for layer L+1 as soon as the router output for L+1 is known, or for the already-known experts of the current layer while compute runs on the hits. Compute threads stay at 4. This is the OS-specific half of the overlap idea: blocked reader threads avoid the oversubscription collapse that naive 6-8 thread pools hit.
- **Bound:** Token 0.29 s = 0.11 s read + 0.18 s compute/sync. Perfect overlap gives max(0.11, 0.18) = 0.18 s -> 0.29/0.18 = 1.61x. Lossless cross-layer prediction only gains 1.00-1.075x (our notes), and current-layer hit-first ordering can hide only part of the misses, so about 40% of reads hide: 0.29 / (0.29 - 0.044) = 1.18x realistic, 1.5x if the router for layer L+1 can be hidden almost fully. Memory-bandwidth contention between DMA and AVX512 kernels lowers this (unmeasured).
- **Cheapest test:** Microbenchmark first, no model: run tools/read_bench.py style 2.7 MB F_NOCACHE preads on 2 threads while a 4-thread AVX512 Q4 matmul loop runs; measure both rates. If read rate stays above 2.5 GB/s and compute above 90% of solo, wire the full patch. Hit-first ordering inside one layer needs no prediction and can be tested directly.
- **Prior art:** Overlap of flash reads and compute is the core of LLM in a flash (arXiv 2312.11514, mobile CPU/GPU, dense FFN with sparsity prediction) and of MoE offloading systems; I did not find a macOS-specific measurement or QoS treatment. Treated as known in principle; the macOS QoS and spin-versus-block detail is the new part.
- **Skeptic (weakened, corrected 1.30x):** The proposed 1.5x upper bound is too high, and "1.5x if the router for layer L+1 can be hidden almost fully" has no support. A corrected bound is about 1.3x on the Qwen3-30B proxy at 66% hit, with 1.1-1.2x realistic. On the 125B target at 16 GB it is about 1.04x. Nothing below was measured. Estimates are labelled.

**Arithmetic from our files**
- M23 gives 353 MiB per token. 353 MiB is about 370 MB, and 370 MB / 3.1 GB/s is 0.119 s of reads.
- The token takes 0.29 s, so non-read time is 0.17 s. The 0.29 / 0.17 = 1.7x bound is the unreachable perfect-overlap case.
- The compute ceiling is about 9 tok/s, which is 0.11 s. So about 0.06 s of the 0.17 s is attention, norms, barriers and sync. That time is not hidden by moving reads.
- The proposal's own bound arithmetic is also inconsistent. 0.29 / (0.29 - 0.044) is 1.18x, but 1.5x is asserted with no derivation.

**What can actually be hidden**
- The reads for layer L cannot start until the router for layer L is known. That router depends on layer L-1's output.
- Our cross-layer prefetch note (lossless gain 1.00-1.075x) therefore applies. Hiding the router for L+1 is not possible without prediction. The "1.5x" case has no support.
- What remains is hit-first ordering inside one layer. Per layer, reads are about 7.4 MiB, or about 2.4 ms (48 layers), against about 3.5 ms of total layer time.
- Assume expert FFN is about 60% of layer time, which is an estimate. That is about 2.1 ms, and the 66% that hit gives about 1.4 ms of compute that can overlap the misses.
- Hideable reads are then about 1.4 of 2.4 ms, or about 55% (0.065 s). That gives 0.29 / 0.225 = 1.29x. This assumes perfect async issue of the up/down misses at routing time.
- The graph runs gate, up and down as separate mul_mat_id ops with barriers between them. Only misses for the first op stall fully, but ordering hits first inside each op is not trivial in ggml.

**Our own simulation already models this lever**
- combo_model.py: t = max(compute, prefetched io) + demand io.
- COMPARISON.md section D: overlap is 1.26x at 8 GB (hit about 0.56) and only 1.03-1.04x at 16-32 GB.
- The 125B target at 16 GB has a hit rate of about 0.77, so the target-relevant gain is about 1.04x. The 1.26x applies to the proxy regime.
- The target also caps at 4.58 tok/s on compute (about 0.22 s). With hit 0.77, reads are smaller, so there is less to hide.

**Contention that lowers the realistic figure (all unmeasured)**
- The 4 compute threads spin in the ggml barrier and occupy all 4 physical cores. A blocked reader that wakes on I/O completion, or a reader on a hyperthread sibling, preempts or slows one compute thread. Every other thread then spins at the barrier.
- This is the same mechanism as the 8-thread collapse (0.26 tok/s). The proposal asserts that blocking readers avoid it but does not show it.
- The 3.1 GB/s was measured with 4 reader threads and 2.7 MB reads. 2 readers may deliver less.
- Compute at about 10 GB/s of streamed weight traffic shares DRAM with the DMA traffic.
- Realistic estimate: 1.1-1.2x on the proxy.

**QoS claim**
- The test machine is an Intel i7-1068NG7. It has no performance/efficiency core split, so QoS class has essentially no effect beyond priority and timer coalescing. The macOS-specific novelty claim is weak.

**Prior art and regime mismatch**
- ProMoE ([arXiv 2410.22134](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.22134v1)) prefetches experts so transfers overlap with layer compute. It reports 1.66x average and 2.07x maximum decode speedup on llama.cpp. That regime is GPU plus CPU memory over PCIe, with learned prediction. It is not CPU-only decode from NVMe with spin barriers.
- HOBBIT ([arXiv 2411.01433](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.01433v2)) reports up to 9.93x, but that is versus other offloading systems and mixes in mixed-precision loading, so it is not an overlap ablation.
- DALI ([arXiv 2602.03495](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.03495v1)) notes prefetch misses cause substantial stall overhead, which supports a cap on what prediction-free overlap hides.
- The llama.cpp barrier history ([PR #9598 commit](https://cdn04132025.gitlink.org.cn/replica/llama.cpp/commit/f0c7b5edf82aa200656fd88c11ae3a805d7130bf) and its related sched_yield removal) confirms the barrier spins. This is consistent with the oversubscription risk above, but no source measures hyperthread collapse directly.
- "SSD Offloading for LLM MoE Weights Considered Harmful in Energy Efficiency" ([arXiv 2508.06978](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.06978)) is a caveat on SSD offloading. I did not read it in detail.

No quality loss, as stated: same bytes, same arithmetic.
  - missed prior art: 
  - decisive test: Run a model-free microbenchmark on the Mac: 2 pthread readers doing 2.7 MB F_NOCACHE preads on the model file (queue depth 2 each), while a 4-thread spinning-barrier AVX512 Q4 matmul loop runs with realistic barrier cadence (about 150 barriers per token, 0.17 s of work). Measure three things. (1) Read GB/s with and without compute; the pass bar is at or above 2.5 GB/s. (2) Compute time per iteration versus solo; the pass bar is within 10%, and barrier-wait variance is the key signal. (3) Wall time per synthetic token, with reads of 0.119 s and compute of 0.17 s, serial versus overlapped. If the overlapped synthetic token is not at or below 0.23 s (about 1.26x), the real patch cannot reach 1.3x. Only then wire hit-first, async issue of up/down misses at routing time. The first real test is a per-layer wall-clock trace of the read stall in the existing pool patch, to confirm how much of the 0.119 s read time falls before the first gate matmul.

### os #2: Pin the non-expert (dense) weights so the pool path stops faulting through mmap
- **Gap:** M22: with the explicit pool at 9 GB wired ballast the run still pages 0.06-0.07 GiB per token via mmap (vs 0.68-1.14 GiB without the pool). Pool median 1.72 tok/s = 0.58 s per token. mmap fault rate is 0.28-0.60 GB/s (M15) or 0.34 GB/s (llama.cpp path, M21) vs 3.11 GB/s explicit. M19 shows resident share of the file falling to 9% under ballast, so attention, norms and output head are evictable.
- **Change:** At load, after mapping, mlock (or pread into wired anonymous memory) every tensor that is not a routed-expert tensor (attention, router, shared parts, embeddings and output head, about 1-1.5 GB on Qwen3-30B, a few GB on the 125B), guarded by a memory budget and ulimit -l (currently unlimited; vm.global_user_wire_limit is 13 GB). Optional env switch, default off.
- **Bound:** Paged bytes per token 0.065 GiB = 0.070 GB. At 0.34 GB/s that is 0.205 s of the 0.58 s token (35%). Best case all of it disappears: 0.58 / (0.58 - 0.205) = 1.55x; with fault cost partly overlapped by other threads, take 0.15 s: 0.58/0.43 = 1.35x. Midpoint about 1.45x. Applies only under memory pressure (ballast or busy neighbours); with no pressure the pages stay resident and the gain is about 1.0x. All numbers are estimates derived from M22/M21/M15.
- **Cheapest test:** Repeat the M22 9 GB ballast A/B (3 alternating pairs, 8 tokens) with arm B = pool + env flag that mlocks non-expert tensors. Record vm_stat Pageins delta and per-process faults per token; success = paged-in bytes per token under 0.01 GiB and median tok/s above 2.2. Null: identical pool arm repeated, to size the pair-to-pair drift seen in M22.
- **Prior art:** llama.cpp --mlock (original commit: disables swapping and compression of model data) pins the whole model, which is impossible when the file is 8x the cache. Pinning only the dense part while experts stream through an explicit pool is not described in anything I found (regime: expert-streaming from disk on a small-RAM host); LLM in a flash (arXiv 2312.11514) keeps attention weights resident in DRAM and streams only FFN rows, the same split but implemented in a custom runtime, no macOS paging data.
- **Skeptic (weakened, corrected 1.25x):** Numbers confirmed from M22, M21 and M19. M22 shows the pool arm paging 0.06-0.07 GiB per token against 0.68-1.14 GiB for plain mmap. Its median is 1.72 tok/s, which is 0.58 s per token, with a range of 1.38-2.02.

1. The 35% share rests on a mismatched rate. The proposal divides 0.070 GB by 0.34 GB/s, but 0.34 GB/s is the effective rate of plain mmap, a different regime from the pool path. Residual dense faults on large tensors may fault faster or slower than that. The 0.205 s figure is therefore an assumption, not a measurement. I could not verify what the 0.065 GiB per token consists of. Whether it is only dense tensors is unproven, because expert-tensor mmap touches, KV cache and graph buffers also fault.

2. The token budget caps the paging time. Compute at the 55 GFLOP/s ceiling is about 0.11 s per token (Qwen3-30B at about 9 tok/s). The pool reads at 1500 MB. At 6 GB (66% hit) the read was about 353 MiB per token, roughly 0.11 s. At 1.5 GB the hit rate is lower, so I assume 0.8-1.0 GB per token, which is 0.26-0.32 s at 3.11 GB/s. I did not measure the 1.5 GB read volume. Compute plus pool I/O comes to about 0.37-0.43 s, which leaves at most about 0.15-0.21 s of the 0.58 s for paging and all other overhead. The best case is then 0.58 / (0.58 - 0.15) = 1.35x, and about 1.25x if some fault cost is already overlapped across threads. The 1.45x midpoint takes the full 0.205 s as pure serial stall, and that is unsupported.

3. Run-to-run drift swamps the effect. M22's spread of 1.38-2.02 tok/s is about plus or minus 20%, and the pairs trended upward, so a gain under about 1.2x cannot be resolved with 3 pairs of 8 tokens.

4. Pinning applies only under memory pressure, and that is the 8x-ratio regime. The wired budget allows it: 9 GB ballast + 1.5 GB pool + about 1 GB dense is about 11.5 GB against a 13 GB limit. On the 125B the dense part is a few GB, so the wire headroom shrinks and the pool must shrink with it. Pinning dense weights then trades pool hit rate against fault avoidance. The net on the 125B target is at most 1.1x and could be negative. The proposal's own estimate says nothing of this.

5. Pinning the dense weights does not change compute. The 125B target remains capped at 4.58 tok/s, so a gain at the proxy's 1.7 tok/s does not carry over proportionally.

6. Prior art. The proposal's claim of no precedent is too strong. Keeping the non-expert weights resident and streaming only the routed experts from SSD is common practice. A search turned up a Jetson write-up that keeps attention, router and shared expert resident, and Flash-MoE on a 48GB MacBook that keeps 5.5GB of non-expert weights resident. I read only secondary summaries of both, not the originals. The FlashMoE paper (arXiv 2601.17063) stores experts and non-experts separately and loads the non-expert part at initialization. I fetched LLM in a flash (arXiv 2312.11514), but its abstract does not mention attention weights, so that comparison is unconfirmed. What looks new is mlock-ing the dense tensors inside a mmap'd llama.cpp alongside an explicit pool. That makes it a minor engineering variant. It is also close to what llama.cpp's existing --mlock achieves when the file fits.

7. Quality and risk. Output is byte-identical. The risk is a wired-memory error or a pool shrink, as in point 4.

I did not verify the pool patch internals, because the rules forbid running the model.
  - missed prior art: 
  - decisive test: Repeat the M22 ballast A/B with at least 5 alternating pairs. The arms are the pool alone (A) and the pool plus an mlock of the non-expert tensors (B). Add a null A-versus-A pair set to size the drift. Before the run, log per-token Pageins, split by tensor class if possible with mincore on the dense ranges, to confirm that the 0.065 GiB is dense. Add per-phase timings from the existing phase-timing patch to measure the real stall time. ### os #3: Neighbour-load aware thread count and QoS (stop the barrier spin from amplifying stolen cores)
- **Gap:** Live sample: another process held 200% CPU (two cores of four) during this session. M23: 4 threads 2.98 tok/s, 2 threads 2.74, 8 threads 0.258 (11x collapse from oversubscription). M05/M21 are marked contended or drifting (M22 notes pair-to-pair trend). A spinning ggml barrier waits for the slowest of 4 threads, so one preempted thread stalls all.
- **Change:** At start and every N tokens read host_cpu_load_info or getloadavg; if other runnable load exceeds 0.5 core, run n_threads = physical cores minus busy cores (3 or 2), and set pthread QoS user-interactive on compute threads (needs no root). Add a pre-run check in the bench harness that refuses to record A/B pairs when foreign CPU exceeds 10%.
- **Bound:** Clean machine: 1.0x (3 threads give about 2.9 vs 2.98, interpolating M23). Contended by one core: 4 threads behave between 4-core and 8-thread-on-4-core, which is 0.26-2.98 tok/s; 3 threads recover about 2.9 tok/s. Even a mild 30% loss from contention gives 1/0.7 = 1.43x recovered; weighting by how often the machine is contended in daily use (estimate 1/3) gives about 1.15x average, 1.3x on a contended run. Pure estimate; the 4-vs-3 thread comparison under a controlled busy loop has not been run.
- **Cheapest test:** One busy-loop process pinned to a share of one core (yes > /dev/null) while running llama-bench -t 4 and -t 3 with the pool, 16 tokens, alternating 3 pairs. Null: same without the busy loop. Record 1-minute load average.
- **Prior art:** llama.cpp discussions say threads spin at 100% even when idle (thread-count guidance: use physical cores); ggml added a low-priority-thread option for contention; nothing found on macOS QoS or runtime adaptation of n_threads to neighbour load.

### os #4: Memory-pressure adaptive pool size (shrink before the OS swaps, grow when free)
- **Gap:** M19: a 9 GB ballast takes plain mmap to 0.23 tok/s; M23: pool size 6000 MB gives 3.46 tok/s but 12000 MB gives 3.30 (no gain, and on 16 GB no room). With swap at 2.1 of 3.0 GB and 6.7 GB free in the live sample, the available budget swings by several GB with other apps. The pool is fixed at launch from an env var.
- **Change:** Size the pool at startup from vm_stat (free + inactive + purgeable minus a floor), and register a dispatch_source MEMORYPRESSURE (warn/critical) plus a periodic kern.memorystatus_level read (live value 73) to release LRU tail slots instead of letting macOS compress or swap them; grow back when pressure clears. Keep non-expert weights pinned (idea 1) so they never fall victim.
- **Bound:** No pressure: 1.0x. Under pressure the failure is a cliff, not a slope (M19 0.65 -> 0.23 tok/s at 9 GB; LRU hit cliff below one token footprint in our notes). Avoiding the swap cliff on a 125B run where pool already fills most RAM could avoid a loss of 1.3-2.8x; weighted by the estimated 20% of runs that hit pressure on a 16 GB machine with a browser open: 1.0 + 0.2 x 1.25 = about 1.25x. Pure estimate; the quantity to measure is time spent in compressor and swap during a 64-token run.
- **Cheapest test:** Log vm_stat Pageouts/Compressions deltas and kern.memorystatus_level around one 6 GB pool run with Chrome and Xcode open versus idle. If Pageouts per run is under 100 MB, the idea is dead (null). Only then build the adaptation.
- **Prior art:** Memory-pressure notifications are standard macOS practice (dispatch memory pressure source); llama.cpp issue 19825 documents the macOS swap death spiral for MoE on a 24 GB M4 Pro (no maintainer fix, closed by author). No adaptive pool for expert streaming found.

### os #5: Mac background IO hygiene: exclude model storage from Spotlight and Time Machine, run the process with default IO policy, and keep disk at low queue contention
- **Gap:** Live: backupd 3.4% CPU, mds 2.1% CPU, / Spotlight index read-only (so no indexing on the system volume). M22 shows pair-to-pair trend (0.24 -> 0.42 mmap, 1.38 -> 2.02 pool), the signature of background state rather than the tool. Explicit reads reach 3.11 GB/s with 4 in flight on an idle disk (M15).
- **Change:** Before each measurement: mdutil -i off on the model volume or touch .metadata_never_index, tmutil addexclusion for the models folder, caffeinate (already on), confirm no Photos/cloud sync via fs_usage -w -f filesys during a 30 s window, and set IO policy IOPOL_IMPORTANT for the reader threads with setiopolicy_np. Record these in every vault run note.
- **Bound:** Reads are about 38% of token time (0.11/0.29 s, M23). A competing background reader halving disk throughput during a read burst would add up to 0.11 s -> 0.40 s token = 1.38x slower; if this occurs 10-20% of the time, the average is 1.04-1.08x, so bound 1.1x. Without a controlled before/after run this is an estimate; the null expectation is 1.0x on a quiet machine.
- **Cheapest test:** fs_usage -w -f diskio for 60 s on an idle machine and then during a 16-token pool run, count foreign bytes. If under 5% of 1 GB/s, it is noise and the idea is closed.
- **Prior art:** Standard macOS performance hygiene (mdutil, tmutil, setiopolicy_np); the Postgres macOS prefetch patch (read) confirms F_RDADVISE exists but gives no benchmark. No application to expert streaming.

### os #6: Allocate the pool as wired superpage memory (2 MB pages, VM_FLAGS_SUPERPAGE_SIZE_2MB) instead of posix_memalign
- **Gap:** Pool is 1.5-6 GB of 4 KB pages via posix_memalign (patch line 124): 1.5 M page-table entries at 6 GB, swappable and compressible under pressure (live compressor: 291 M compressions, 2.1 GB swap in use). Expert tensors are streamed once per hit and are not compressible (zlib-1 ratio 0.977, measured here).
- **Change:** Allocate the pool with mmap(VM_FLAGS_SUPERPAGE_SIZE_2MB) (Intel macOS), which is wired by construction, so it cannot be compressed or swapped, and cuts TLB misses for 0.9 MB expert streaming; fall back to mlock on plain pages if the superpage allocation fails (needs contiguous physical memory; may fail on a fragmented 16 GB system). Round expert slots to 2 MB multiples (slot waste about 0.1-1.1 MB per 0.9 MB tensor, so slot size should hold all three tensors of one expert, 2.7 MB -> 4 MB, 33% waste, so test slot packing first).
- **Bound:** TLB effect: per token about 3.3 GB of weight reads streamed sequentially; one page walk per 4 KB at roughly 20-30 cycles amortised over 4 KB of 64 B lines is under 1% of the memory time; assume 2-4% at most. Compressor/swap effect: zero when free memory is ample (current sample shows 6.7 GB free). Combined upper bound 1.07x under pressure, 1.02x idle. Estimates only.
- **Cheapest test:** Micro: 4-thread streaming dot-product over 3 GB with 4 KB pages vs 2 MB superpages (small C program, 20 s). If less than 3%, drop the TLB part and keep only mlock of the pool (cheaper variant, same swap protection). Then run the pool at 6 GB while a 10 GB wired ballast is present and compare vm_stat compressions delta per token.
- **Prior art:** Apple's mmap(2) superpage flag is Intel-only and documented in the kernel headers; PostgreSQL and database engines use huge pages on Linux (transparent or explicit) for buffer pools. Not found applied to LLM weight streaming on macOS.

### os #7: Compressed or swapped expert tier via the macOS compressor (null test, proposed and killed)
- **Gap:** Live compressor already holds 1.93 M pages (7.9 GB logical) at 1.6 GB resident, ratio about 4.8:1 for ordinary app data. Pool hit rate is 66% at 6 GB and rises only a few points per extra GB (3.30 at 12 GB, same hits as 6 GB in a 16-token run).
- **Change:** Idea: store evicted experts in compressed RAM so a miss costs a decompress instead of a 0.9 MB SSD read. Test is a pure measurement of compressibility on the weights.
- **Bound:** Measured ratio 0.977 with zlib-1 on 80 MB sampled across the OLMoE Q4_K_M file (0.976 with zlib-6; scratchpad angle_os/c.py). Capacity gain 1/0.977 = 1.023x; pool hit rate increase for 2.3% more bytes is below 1 point; token gain under 0.5%. Even a perfect compressor does not help, and the OS compressor spends CPU for nothing, which is exactly the reason to wire the pool (idea 4).
- **Cheapest test:** Already done (a few seconds of Python). To be thorough, repeat on Qwen3-30B once the 18.6 GB file is local; expected within 0.96-1.0.
- **Prior art:** Weight compression for LLMs relies on lower-bit quantisation, not generic entropy coding, which is known to give only a few percent on Q4_K data (ZipNN-style entropy coding targets BF16/FP16 exponents, not K-quant blocks; not independently verified here).


## Angle: workload

Main finding: once explicit reads make decode fast, prefill dominates wall time for chat and agent use, so TTFT levers outrank decode levers. Estimate for the 125B target: 12 GFLOP/token at about 140 GFLOP/s batched gives about 0.086 s/token, so a 2k-token prompt costs about 172 s against about 80 s for 200 decoded tokens at 2.5 tok/s. Measured here from the decode traces: the expert union over k consecutive tokens per layer is 8.0 (k=1), 12.3-12.7 (k=2), 19.1-20.0 (k=4) and 27.5-30.1 (k=8) of 128 on story/code/db, so verifying k tokens reads far fewer bytes than k separate steps (about 0.6x at k=4). Consecutive-token expert overlap is only 0.42-0.46. Scripts are in <scratch> (u.py, s.py). Sources read in full: MoESD (https://arxiv.org/html/2505.19645v4, expert-activation model N(t)=E(1-((E-K)/E)^t) and its memory-bound-to-compute-bound threshold) and Layered Prefill (https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.08055, chunked prefill reloads experts, up to 39% extra traffic). ### workload #1: Persistent on-disk KV / prefix cache (save and restore prompt state across turns and sessions)
- **Gap:** Prefill share of wall time is 181 s of 702 s (26%) on the VPS (COMPARISON.md row 3), and it grows once decode gets explicit reads. Estimate for 125B: about 0.086 s per prompt token (12 GFLOP at about 140 GFLOP/s batched, extrapolated from OLMoE prefill 279 vs decode 107 GFLOP/s, M16). An 8k-token agent system prompt plus tool schema is about 700 s of TTFT on every cold start. KV size from deep_P12a.json is 0.36 MiB/token for the large model.
- **Change:** Hash the token prefix, store KV state to NVMe after the first prefill, and on a match read it back with the explicit pool path (sequential, 3.1 GB/s) and prefill only the new tail. Keep the in-RAM prefix check for within-session turns and add disk persistence for cross-session reuse.
- **Bound:** 8192 tokens x 0.36 MiB = 2.9 GB, read at 3.1 GB/s = 0.95 s versus 8192 x 0.086 = 705 s recompute. Turn wall time with a 300-token reply at about 2.5 tok/s (120 s): (705+120)/(1+120) = 6.8x for a fully static prefix. Rounded to 8x as the ceiling. Real agent turns also add new tool output, so the realistic gain is lower (estimate).
- **Cheapest test:** On the Mac with Qwen3-30B (about 0.1 s/token prefill): llama.cpp prompt-cache save and load of a 4k prefix. Compare TTFT (cold prefill) with the TTFT of a KV restore, check byte-identical greedy output, and measure restore time at the 3 GB/s read rate.
- **Prior art:** llama.cpp --prompt-cache and server slot save/restore; vLLM automatic prefix caching; SGLang RadixAttention (https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.07104); LMCache style KV offload to disk. Our own P12a notes cover RAM-side prefix match.
- **Skeptic (weakened, corrected 3.50x):** The mechanism is sound and exact, but the 8x ceiling does not survive recomputation from our files, and the gain over what already exists is much smaller.

1. Prefill cost is overstated about 2x. The proposal uses 140 GFLOP/s batched, which is half the decode-to-prefill rate. M16 gives 279-412 GFLOP/s batched prefill (311 used for TTFT elsewhere). At 12 GFLOP/token and 311 GFLOP/s that is 0.039 s/token, so 8192 tokens cost about 316 s, not 705 s. A disk floor applies as well: a 512-token chunk activates nearly every expert in every layer, so each chunk reads about 69 GB at 3.1 GB/s = 22 s, or 0.043 s/token. That is about 352 s for 8k tokens, and the P12a note records 4-6x extra disk bytes for small chunks. A realistic cold prefill is therefore about 350-400 s, not 705 s. (Estimate, not measured on the 125B.)

2. The 0.36 MiB/token figure is not from our target model. deep_P12a.md takes it from a user log of a "GLM-4.6/R1 class" model on 2x3090 (7,745 tokens = 2,783 MiB). Our 125B has 48 layers, so its KV size is unknown. The restore time is correspondingly unmeasured, though 1-3 s is still negligible next to recompute.

3. RAM competition is ignored. The restored 2.9 GB of KV sits in 16 GB of RAM and comes out of the expert cache. The LRU hit cliff and the 66% to 77% hit-rate regime are sensitive to that. A 10-15% decode slowdown is a plausible estimate, not a measurement. Decode attention over 8k tokens also adds a little.

4. Most of the idea is already implemented. Within-session reuse is in llama.cpp slots and --cache-ram. P12a notes llama.cpp already has slot save/restore to disk (/slots save and restore), with 67.9 MB for 354 tokens saved in 67 ms and restored in 28 ms. So the incremental contribution is only cross-session cold start of a fixed prefix. Agent turns with new tool output gain about 1.0x over the existing in-RAM prefix check.

5. A reported restore bug exists. After a restart, restore did not produce cache hits (cache_n 0) in the same discussion. Byte-identical output is therefore unverified.

6. Published results contradict the "disk beats recompute" assumption in some regimes. The py-kvcache paper (arXiv 2609.11744) reports an 8k-token prompt needing about 78% prefix reuse to break even on an H100 against a disk-backed connector. Another paper (arXiv 2606.09441) and the Sogang LMCache thesis show disk hits can lose to recompute when compute is fast relative to disk. Those are GPU regimes. On our CPU, compute is slow (about 0.04 s/token) and the disk is fast relative to it, so the idea still wins, but by a smaller margin. The llm-d docs also say local disk is the slowest tier. The cited SGLang RadixAttention work is GPU and in-memory, so it does not support the disk claim.

Corrected arithmetic, 125B, 8k static prefix, cross-session only, 300-token reply:
- Cold: 352 s prefill + 300 tokens at about 2.5 tok/s (120 s) = 472 s.
- Restore: about 1 s read + 120 s x 1.15 (smaller expert cache) = 139 s.
- Ratio 472/139 = 3.4x, rounded to 3.5x. This applies only to the first turn of a later session with a byte-identical prefix. It is about 1.0x for turns inside a session, and for any workload with a mostly new prompt.

On the Qwen3-30B proxy, prefill is about 0.1 s/token, so 4k tokens cost 400 s. Restoring about 0.1-0.4 GB of KV takes under 1 s.

Novelty is known and the code largely exists. Value is real but narrow.
  - missed prior art: 
  - decisive test: On the Mac with Qwen3-30B, run llama-server with a 4k fixed system prompt. Measure (a) cold TTFT, (b) TTFT after /slots restore in a fresh process, and (c) a within-session --cache-ram hit. Check that cache_n equals the prefix length after the restart, that greedy output is byte-identical, and that restore time matches file size divided by 3 GB/s. Also run decode with the restored KV resident under the 9 GB ballast and compare tok/s with the no-KV baseline. That decode comparison quantifies the expert-cache loss. Separately, count per-chunk disk bytes during 512-token prefill to replace the 0.043 s/token disk-floor estimate with a measurement.

### workload #2: N-gram / prompt-lookup speculation with a union-aware verify batch (also the slot for MTP heads)
- **Gap:** Decode is compute-bound at 55 GFLOP/s (M10: 8.3 tok/s x 2 x 3.3B), while batched kernels run about 2.6x faster per token (OLMoE 279 vs 107 GFLOP/s), so a verify batch of k tokens costs less than k single steps. Our measured expert union per layer (traces, 128 experts) is 12.3-12.7 for k=2, 19.1-20.0 for k=4, 27.5-30.1 for k=8, versus 8.0 for k=1, so k=4 reads 2.4-2.5x the bytes of one token, not 4x. Consecutive-token overlap is only 0.42-0.46, so verification brings more misses than a single step.
- **Change:** Draft with prompt lookup (match the last n generated tokens against the context, propose the following 3-4 tokens; free on CPU, no draft model) and verify all of them in one batched forward pass. Add a gate: only speculate when the n-gram match is long (agent workloads copy file contents, JSON and code from context). Order the verify reads union-first so each expert is read once per step, and drop a draft token early if its experts are not cached and the match is short. MTP heads, if the 125B model has one, would use the same verify path.
- **Bound:** Per-token step now about 0.29 s at 66% hit (0.11 s IO + 0.11 s compute + about 0.07 other). A k=4 verify step: compute 4/2.6 x 0.11 = 0.17 s; IO scales with the union, 2.4 x 0.11 = 0.26 s (worst case, ignoring that the union raises hit rate); other 0.07, so about 0.5 s if serialised, which is 1.7x the cost of one step. Break-even needs 1.7 accepted tokens per step. If 3 of 4 drafts are accepted on copy-heavy spans, 4 tokens per 0.5 s = 8 tok/s versus 3.46, which is 2.3x, rounded down to 2.0x with draft failures. On non-copy text (accept <1) the gate must turn it off, so the whole-session gain is likely 1.1-1.3x (estimate).
- **Cheapest test:** Fully in RAM first (removes IO): run llama.cpp's n-gram lookup speculation on Qwen3-30B at 4 threads on one code-edit prompt and a story prompt, and record accepted-per-step and tok/s. Then repeat under the 9 GB ballast with the explicit pool. Decisive number: tok/s ratio and accepted tokens per step for each workload type.
- **Prior art:** Prompt lookup decoding (https://github.com/apoorvumang/prompt-lookup-decoding, 2-4x on input-grounded tasks); llama.cpp lookup / ngram speculation; speculative decoding on MoE: MoESD https://arxiv.org/html/2505.19645v4 (verification cost nearly flat once t exceeds the full-activation threshold; regime is GPU batch >= 16 so does not carry to batch 1 and top-8 of 128); SP-MoE https://arxiv.org/html/2510.10302v2, DraftExpert https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.24434 and AcceptMoE (cache-aware verifier expert sets, 2.06x under offloading on a GPU); https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.11580 (gain tracks output/context overlap).
- **Skeptic (weakened, corrected 1.30x):** Union numbers reproduce from the traces (I recomputed them with non-overlapping windows): k=1 is 8.0; k=2 is 12.3-12.7; k=4 is 19.1-20.0; k=8 is 27.5-30.1. The claim that k=4 reads about 2.4-2.5x the bytes of one token is therefore correct.

I could not check the 0.29 s token split (0.11 IO, 0.11 compute, 0.07 other) against the files. COMPARISON.md does not exist at the path given, so I took the numbers from the task text.

Weaknesses:
1. The 2.6x batched-kernel speedup (OLMoE, 279 vs 107 GFLOP/s) is applied to the wrong batch shape. In a k=4 verify, the 32 token-expert pairs fall on about 19.8 distinct experts per layer. That is only about 1.6 tokens per expert, so the weight-reuse gain is nowhere near 2.6x. With a more realistic 1.3-1.5x, compute is 4/1.4 x 0.11 = 0.31 s, not 0.17 s.
2. Corrected k=4 step: 0.31 compute + 0.26 IO + 0.07 other (this term also grows with k, through attention and the lm_head) = about 0.64 s, or 2.2x one step. That puts break-even at about 2.2 accepted tokens per step, not 1.7.
3. Best case, with all 3 drafts accepted (4 tokens per step): 4/0.64 = 6.3 tok/s against 3.46, which is 1.8x. The proposal's 2.3x, rounded to 2.0x, relied on the optimistic compute term. It also overlooked that tokens past the first draft hit experts outside the 66% cached set, because consecutive-token overlap is only 0.42-0.46. IO may therefore exceed the union-scaled 0.26 s.
4. The whole-session gain is capped by the share of copy-heavy tokens. At 25% of tokens in accept-3 spans, with the gate costing nothing elsewhere: time per token = 0.75 x 0.29 + 0.25 x (0.64/4) = 0.2175 + 0.04 = 0.2575 s, so 1.13x. At 40% copy share it is about 1.25x. A ceiling of 1.3x is generous. The copy share is an unmeasured estimate.
5. Cascade (arXiv 2506.20675) reports that on MoE models, speculation raises data movement and verification time by 2-3x and can slow decoding by up to 1.5x on some workloads. It proposes disabling speculation when it hurts. That matches our union arithmetic and supports the need for the gate. Its regime is GPU serving in vLLM with HBM as the bottleneck, so it does not carry over directly to CPU with an NVMe cache. The newer papers MoE-Spec and EcoSpec (found in the search, not read) make the same point: verification cost depends on the expert union, and they fix it by capping or costing the experts loaded. They are also GPU work.
6. On the 125B target the compute ceiling is 4.58 tok/s. It has 512 experts per layer and top-10, so the union grows faster and the batched compute gain is smaller still. The proxy therefore overstates the gain.
7. Prompt lookup decoding itself reports 2-4x only on input-grounded tasks, mostly with dense models. Greedy verification is lossless, but sampled decoding accepts fewer tokens.

Net: the idea is real but narrow. It gives about 1.8x on pure-copy spans and about 1.1-1.3x over a whole session. It is not 2x.
  - missed prior art: 
  - decisive test: In RAM, with no IO, run llama.cpp n-gram lookup speculation on Qwen3-30B at 4 threads. Measure (a) the time of a k=4 batched forward pass against 4 single steps, to get the real compute scaling for a MoE batch, and (b) accepted tokens per step on a code-edit prompt and a story prompt. Then repeat under the 9 GB ballast with the explicit pool. Proceed only if the k=4 step is at most 1.7x one step on the ballasted run and accepted tokens per step are at least 2.2 on the target workload.

### workload #3: Grammar / schema jump-forward in tool-call decoding (forced tokens batched, not decoded)
- **Gap:** Agent output is mostly tool calls: JSON keys, braces and fixed argument names are fully determined by the schema. Each such token currently pays a full decode step (0.29 s on 30B at 66% hit, estimated 0.4 s on 125B), though the grammar already knows it. No measurement of the forced-token fraction in our notes.
- **Change:** When the grammar mask allows exactly one token (or a fixed string), append the whole forced run to the context and process it as one small prefill batch (via the same verify path as idea 3), with no per-token sampling and no per-token expert read for those positions beyond the union. Needs a retokenisation guard at the boundary.
- **Bound:** If a fraction f of generated tokens is forced, steps drop from N to N(1-f) plus one batch per run. With f=0.4 (estimate; typical compact JSON tool call) and a run cost of about 1.7 single steps per 4 tokens at k=4, the time is 0.6N + 0.1N x 1.7 = 0.77N, i.e. 1.3x; the ceiling at zero batch cost is 1/(1-0.4) = 1.67x, quoted as 1.5x. Applies only to constrained (tool-call) output, not prose.
- **Cheapest test:** Take 20 recorded tool-call transcripts (any agent logs), tokenise the JSON with the model tokenizer and count tokens where a JSON grammar leaves a single legal token; that fraction f gives the bound with no model run.
- **Prior art:** SGLang compressed finite state machines / jump-forward decoding (https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.07104); XGrammar; llama.cpp has GBNF grammars but no jump-forward. Regime there is GPU serving, where a forced token costs a full memory-bound step as well.

### workload #4: Prefill ubatch sizing plus layer-sequential expert streaming overlapped with batched compute
- **Gap:** A prefill ubatch of 512 tokens touches about every expert of every layer (MoESD model: 512 tokens x top-8 of 128 gives 128 of 128 per layer, expected 100%). Each ubatch pass therefore streams the whole model: 18.6 GB at 3.11 GB/s explicit = 6.0 s versus 55 s at mmap 0.34 GB/s; for 125B 69 GB = 22 s per pass. The VPS prompt eval was 181 s, and madvise only gained 1.30x on it (139 s). Compute per 512-token pass on 125B is about 44 s (estimate). If read and compute are serialised, a pass costs 66 s.
- **Change:** For prefill only: (a) read each layer's whole expert tensors sequentially with large reads, a layer ahead of compute, because the full set is known in advance and no routing prediction is needed; (b) raise the ubatch so reads amortise (1024-2048) and the read is hidden under compute; (c) skip the LRU for prefill reads (stream through a small ring buffer rather than polluting the decode cache).
- **Bound:** Prefill goes from IO+compute = 22+44 = 66 s per 512-token pass to max(22,44) = 44 s, which is 1.5x on prefill. A 2k-token prompt costs about 172 s, plus 200 tokens decode at 2.5 tok/s = 80 s, so wall = 252 s. With prefill/1.5 = 115 s, wall = 195 s, which is 1.29x overall (all figures are estimates). On the 30B proxy (6 s read vs 5 s compute per pass) the same overlap gives at most 1.9x on prefill.
- **Cheapest test:** On the Mac with the explicit read pool: time llama-bench pp512 with -ub 128/512/1024/2048 under 9 GB ballast, reading the phase timing patch output. Then add a one-layer-ahead sequential prefetch thread and compare. Decisive number: prefill seconds per 1k tokens versus the compute-only bound (model fully in RAM).
- **Prior art:** Layered prefill (https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.08055: chunked prefill reloads experts, up to 39% extra traffic, TTFT down to 70%); FlexGen-style layer pipelining; KTransformers/MoE-Lightning for CPU-GPU pipelining. Regime there is GPU serving with co-located decode, not a single user with NVMe-resident experts.

### workload #5: Idle-time warm-up between agent turns (use tool-execution seconds to stage the next turn's working set and KV)
- **Gap:** Agent loops spend seconds to minutes in tool execution (compile, tests, search) during which disk (3.1 GB/s) and CPU are idle, but the engine does nothing, so the next turn's prefill starts cold. Not in our notes: no note names between-turn idle as a resource. Prefill then streams the full model (6 s on the 30B proxy, about 22 s on 125B per ubatch pass) with the page cache evicted by the tool's own I/O.
- **Change:** While the tool runs, (a) restore/keep the prefix KV in RAM (idea 1) and (b) pre-read the layer-0..k experts and the hot decode set into the pool so the next prefill's first layers hit; stop at once when the tool returns or when memory pressure appears. The speculative part is bounded because prefill reads everything anyway, so only the first passes and the decode-hot set matter.
- **Bound:** Per-turn work on 125B (estimate): prefill of 500 new tokens = 43 s compute, plus one pass of whole-model reads (22 s) serialised = 65 s, then decode 100 tokens at 2.5 tok/s = 40 s. Total 105 s. If the idle window is >= 22 s and hides the first pass's reads by pre-reading the hot set (cache holds about 77% hits, so about 12 GB hot of 69 GB, 4 s read) and prefix KV (idea 1), saved time is about 4 s IO plus ~10 s first-pass stall, i.e. up to 14 s of 105 s, which is 1.15x. Zero if tool calls last less than a few seconds.
- **Cheapest test:** Log gaps between model calls in 10 recorded agent sessions (timestamps from any agent harness) and compute the fraction with gap > 10 s; with that fraction and the read rates above, the bound follows with no model run. Then a 30B test: time TTFT with and without a pre-read of the hot set after a 30 s idle period under ballast.
- **Prior art:** Not found for local single-user MoE streaming. Related: speculative prefetch during the draft stage in SP-MoE (https://arxiv.org/html/2510.10302v2), and KV prefetch/offload schemes in LMCache, but those target GPU serving, not idle windows between turns.

### workload #6: Quantise the KV cache (q8_0 / q4_0) to give the expert cache the RAM it needs at long context
- **Gap:** At 16 GB total, the expert cache competes with KV. From P12a: KV = 0.36 MiB/token for the large model, so 8k context = 2.9 GB, which is 18% of 16 GB, and the hit-rate cliff sits below one token footprint. Public benchmark suggests 16 GB hit about 0.77; our 125B projections (COMPARISON.md section C) assume no KV pressure.
- **Change:** Run K and V in q8_0 (or q4_0 V) and size the pool after the KV allocation; also cap context at what the task needs. Flash attention is required for a quantised V in llama.cpp.
- **Bound:** q8 halves KV: 8k tokens x 0.36 MiB = 2.9 GB to 1.45 GB frees 1.45 GB for experts. Assume (estimate) hit gain of about 0.02 per GB near hit 0.77, so hit goes to about 0.80 and the miss rate falls from 0.23 to 0.20, which is -13% IO. With IO about 40% of token time, time falls 5%, i.e. 1.05-1.06x. At 32k context the freed RAM is 5.8 GB and the gain would be much larger, but that regime is not in our proxy.
- **Cheapest test:** On the Mac, 30B with 9 GB ballast: run with an 8k context filled, f16 KV versus q8_0 KV, with the explicit pool sized to what is left; compare hit rate and tok/s. Also check perplexity with q8 KV.
- **Prior art:** llama.cpp --cache-type-k/v; KIVI and KVQuant (https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.02750) for KV quantisation quality. The interaction with an expert cache budget is not covered in the sources I saw.

### workload #7: Seed the decode expert cache from the prefill routing profile (instead of the prefill's last-ubatch residue)
- **Gap:** After prefill, the cache holds whatever the last ubatch streamed through, not the experts the reply will use. Measured on traces (decode tokens 0-31 as a stand-in for a prefill profile, top-C per layer by frequency, LRU C=41/128 per layer, about 6 GB for 30B): hit over the first 16 decode tokens goes from 0.64/0.64/0.68 cold (story/code/db) to 0.80/0.80/0.86 seeded; at C=24 0.56-0.59 to 0.62-0.65. Over the full run the gain is only +0.004 to +0.012.
- **Change:** Use the prefill routing counts (we already have the prefill trace patch) per layer to choose the cache contents at the start of decode: pin or load the top-C by prompt frequency before the first generated token, and let LRU take over after.
- **Bound:** Miss rate in the first 16 tokens drops from about 0.36 to 0.20 at C=41, i.e. 0.16 x 1.04 GB/token (0.34 x 3.1 GB/token... per-token footprint about 1.04 GB, estimated from 353 MiB at 34% miss) = about 0.17 GB saved per token x 16 tokens = 2.7 GB = 0.9 s at 3.1 GB/s. On a 200-token reply taking about 58 s that is 1.5%, so 1.02-1.04x at best. Larger on short replies (e.g. 16-token tool calls: 0.9 s of about 5 s = up to 1.2x).
- **Cheapest test:** No model run: replay the traces with the first N tokens standing in for a prompt profile, N=32/64, and C swept over the cache sizes of interest; to make it real, collect one prefill trace with the existing patch and compare it to the decode trace of the same prompt for overlap.
- **Prior art:** Same-prompt pin was measured by us (COMPARISON row 5: 1.9x same-prompt, 1.3x cross-prompt, but pin load cost 60 s and no net gain on long runs). Profile-guided expert placement is in published offloading work (e.g. ProMoE caching https://arxiv.org/html/2410.22134v1), but I found no paper that seeds the decode cache from the same request's prefill routing.


## Angle: layout

Layout is a small lever for decode on this machine and a moderate one for prefill. Decode: the token is 0.289 s, of which about 0.11-0.12 s is reads (353 MiB at 3.11 GB/s) and the rest is compute and sync, so any byte or read-count cut is diluted to 1.03-1.08x (M23, M15). Prefill: nobody in the notes has priced the kernel-interleaved layout. Mainline llama.cpp reports +54-76% prefill and -2% decode for Q4_K interleaving (AVX2, Ryzen; Q4_K_M 45.8 -> 73.8 pp512), and +26-34% more from the AVX512 path. We turn this off with --repack 0 (F01) and never measured it. An offline-repacked file would give the gain without the load-time copy that defeats mmap. Prior art on the other layout ideas is already in our notes: SGLang Expert Pack (one aligned block per expert), and flash-moe's own "clustering 0% benefit". Ran three new trace analyses (scratchpad angle_layout/t.py, u.py, v.py) on the 3 real routing traces with a global LRU of 2098 experts (6000 MB / 2.86 MB). Results:
(a) The cold half of experts, ranked on the other two prompts, takes 12-30% of accesses and 26-33% of misses. Misses are not concentrated in cold experts, so frequency-stratified bit allocation bounds at about 1.06x.
(b) The best-successor next-layer expert is on the same miss list for only 8-12% of misses, so cross-layer bundling is about 1.005x.
(c) Seeding the pool with a hot-first file of the 1000 top experts (ranked on the other prompts) saves only 0.5-0.62 misses per seeded expert, so it cannot pay for its own read. Net IO moves from 2.6 s to 2.8 s over 32 tokens.
Also found: Qwen3-30B Q4_K_M uses Q6_K down projections in 24 of 48 layers, which makes an expert 3.06 MB in those layers and 2.65 MB in the others (average 2.86 MB). Per-layer demotion of those layers is the one layout-side lever that is a pure file change. Caveat on every bound: serial IO plus compute, 4 threads, no overlap built yet. If overlap is built, byte-saving levers drop to about 1.00x at 16 GB and above. Nothing was run on a model (read-only rules).

### layout #1: Offline kernel-interleaved expert layout (pre-repacked Q4_K, aligned, written once)
- **Gap:** Notes only record 'probe --repack 0' (Fixes/F01): llama.cpp repack allocates a CPU_REPACK buffer that defeats mmap, so we switched it off and never measured the repack benefit on the Mac (grep of Measurements/Runs shows no repack-on vs repack-off result). Prefill on the Mac is compute-bound: OLMoE pp512 107.4 tok/s = 279 GFLOP/s vs decode 107 GFLOP/s (M16); Qwen3-30B decode 55 GFLOP/s (M10). A 500-token prompt on the 30B proxy is roughly 3.3 TFLOP = 12 s of compute (estimate from M16 rate) plus about 5 s of reads. Published: mainline Q4_K 8-row interleave gives pp512 +54..+76% and tg128 -2% (Ryzen 7600X AVX2); the AVX512 path adds +26..+34% pp512 and tg -0.1% (Ryzen 9600X).
- **Change:** One-time conversion tool that writes each expert's Q4_K tensors in the interleaved block order the CPU kernel wants (same byte count, so the file does not grow), expert-aligned at 4 KiB, with a GGUF type flag so ggml selects the interleaved kernel without a runtime repack. Works with the explicit pread pool because pool slots hold ready-to-use bytes. The 24 Q6_K down layers stay as they are (Q6_K has no interleaved kernel in mainline). In-place conversion layer by layer needs about 1.4 GB of scratch, not 69 GB: the Mac showed 33 GiB free against a 69 GB file.
- **Bound:** Wall time for a 500-token prompt plus 40 generated tokens on the 30B proxy, serial: prefill compute 12 s + prefill reads 5.4 s (0.9 x 18.6 GB / 3.1 GB/s) + decode 40/3.46 = 11.6 s = 29 s. Interleaved kernels cover Q4_K only: gate+up plus the Q4_K down layers = about 83% of expert FLOPs. Prefill speedup S = 1/(0.17 + 0.83/1.75) = 1.55 at the optimistic 1.75x kernel gain (1.54 x 1.14 compounded from the two PRs, different CPUs). Prefill compute 12 -> 7.7 s, total 29 -> 24.7 s = 1.17x; with no Q6_K exclusion 12 -> 6.9 s, total 23.9 s = 1.21x. Decode term unchanged (published -0.1..-2%). Longer prompts approach the full prefill bound 1.55x; a short chat prompt gives about 1.0x. All prefill numbers are estimates.
- **Cheapest test:** On the Mac, in-RAM Qwen3-30B (or the OLMoE file already in runs/bench/mac): llama-bench -p 512 -n 64 -t 4 with --repack 0 vs default repack on, 3 repetitions each, record pp512 and tg64 (about 5 minutes, no ballast). If pp512 gain < 1.15x on i7-1068NG7 (AVX512+VNNI) or the MoE expert path is not repacked in this llama.cpp build (check PR 12544), kill the idea.
- **Prior art:** Mainline llama.cpp Q4_K block interleaving https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/pull/12332 (AVX2, pp512 +54-76%, tg -2%, load time of DeepSeek R1 Q4_K_S 25.7 s -> 500 s because it repacks at load) and AVX512 follow-up https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/pull/12829 (pp512 +26-34%, tg -0.1%); ik_llama.cpp _R4/_R8 row-interleaved types https://github.com/ikawrakow/ik_llama.cpp ; SGLang Expert Pack offline repack https://lmsys.org/blog/2026-08-29-sglang-ssd-expert-pack/. Not published: shipping the interleaved bytes inside the on-disk MoE file to avoid the load-time copy for an expert-streaming pool.
- **Skeptic (weakened, corrected 1.10x):** The direction is right: kernel interleaving helps prefill only, and decode stays flat. The 1.2x bound is inflated, and the idea costs more than "cheap".

1. The kernel gain is not compounded correctly. PR 12332 (https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/pull/12332) measured +61% to +76% pp512 on Ryzen 7600X, on dense Llama-2 7B. PR 12829 (https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/pull/12829) measured +26% to +34% on a Ryzen 9600X, again on dense Llama-2 7B. The PR 12829 baseline is already the AVX2-repacked kernel, and its absolute baseline was 66.8 t/s against 45.8 for PR 12332. Multiplying 1.54 by 1.14 therefore mixes two CPUs and two baselines, and the 1.75x kernel gain is unsupported.
   - PR 12829 does not mention VNNI or Ice Lake client and does not cover MoE. Nothing published covers the i7-1068NG7.
   - A defensible kernel gain for the Q4_K matmuls on this CPU is 1.2-1.5x.

2. The regime is mismatched. Both PRs benchmark dense models at large batch.
   - In MoE, a 500-token prompt with top-8 of 128 experts gives about 500*8/128 = 31 rows per expert. That is a small-batch GEMM, where interleaving gains are smaller. The PR 12332 comments show the 20-token case gaining less.
   - PR 12332 originally did not touch the MoE path. It was added later in PR 12544, and I could not confirm the details.
   - Our llama.cpp work copy's repack.cpp does have forward_mul_mat_id for Q4_K_8x8, but only when the tensor lives in the CPU_REPACK buffer type and the activations are F32. That buffer is exactly what F01 turned off.

3. The "kernel-selecting GGUF type flag" is not cheap. In the current code the repack happens inside the repack buffer's set_tensor, and the kernel is chosen from a tensor-traits object attached to that buffer.
   - There is no on-disk interleaved Q4_K type.
   - A new ggml type, or a bypass of the repack buffer type with a type-trait hack, is needed. It must also work with the explicit read pool slots and the 3D expert tensors.
   - That is a patch of at least a few hundred lines, not a conversion script.

4. The serial arithmetic mixes regimes. I recomputed it from the idea's own numbers.
   - Prefill compute is 3.3 B active * 2 * 500 = 3.3 TFLOP, so 3.3e12 / 279e9 = 11.8 s. That matches the idea's 12 s.
   - Reads are about 5.4 s. If reads overlap with compute, prefill time is max(compute, reads), so only the compute reduction matters while compute-bound.
   - With a kernel gain of 1.3x on the 83% of FLOPs that are Q4_K, S = 1/(0.17 + 0.83/1.3) = 1.24. Prefill compute falls from 11.8 s to 9.5 s.
   - Total with 40 generated tokens (taking the idea's 11.6 s decode): 29 s becomes about 26.7 s, which is 1.09x. At the optimistic 1.75x kernel gain it is 1.17x, so the idea's 1.2x needs the optimistic case plus no Q6_K exclusion.

5. On the target workload the effect is near zero. The 125B model decodes at 4.58 tok/s at best. For chat-length prompts (under 100 tokens) the gain is about 1.0x, and decode is unchanged (-0.1% to -2% published).

6. Baseline uncertainty. M16 (OLMoE Q4_K_M pp512 107.4 tok/s, 279 GFLOP/s) does not record whether repack was on.
   - Q3_K_S has no repack path yet reached 158 tok/s, 1.47x the Q4_K_M figure. That suggests Q4_K_M was either already repacked or has a weak unrepacked baseline.
   - If M16 already ran with repack on, the offline layout adds nothing for in-RAM runs. The only benefit is relative to the repack-off streaming baseline, which has not been measured.

7. Quality risk is none, which holds. Published perplexity was 5.8898 vs 5.8887.

I read both PRs. I did not verify the SGLang page or the ik_llama.cpp types.
  - missed prior art: 
  - decisive test: On the Mac, with Qwen3-30B Q4_K_M fully in RAM, run `llama-bench -p 512 -n 64 -t 4 -r 3` with `--repack 0` and with repack on, using a non-mmap load so the repack buffer is used. Run `-p 128` as well to match the 31 rows per expert case. Before the run, confirm the MoE expert tensors are actually in the CPU_REPACK buffer. Check the build's AVX512 flags and the buffer type in the load log, and look at whether M16 was run with repack on.

Kill the idea if pp512 gain is below 1.15x. In that case the real ceiling is about 1.05x. If the gain is 1.3x or more, the 500-token end-to-end bound is about 1.1x, and building a new on-disk type is justified only for long-prompt use.

### layout #2: One aligned extent per expert: single pread, 4 KiB start, optional pair coalescing
- **Gap:** Stock file: gate and up of one expert are 114 MB apart and down is a third tensor, so an expert is 3 reads (1152 reads per token vs 384 repacked, B.md). No expert slice starts on a 4 KiB boundary (tensor data is 32-byte aligned) although every slice length is already a 4 KiB multiple (884,736 = 216 pages; 1,290,240 = 315 pages). Measured read rates (M15): 0.9 MB x 4 threads 2.69 GB/s, 2.7 MB 3.11, 4.0 MB 3.42. Pool run (M23): 353 MiB per token = 0.119 s at 3.11 GB/s out of 0.289 s per token.
- **Change:** Write the expert file as expert-major records [gate|up|down] padded to 4 KiB, with an offset table; pool does one pread per miss into an aligned slot, no trimming. Optionally lay out experts in co-activation chains so adjacent misses merge into 4+ MB reads.
- **Bound:** IO share 0.119 s. Three-tensor reads behave like the 0.9 MB x4 row (2.69 GB/s) vs 3.11 GB/s for the single 2.7 MB read: IO time x(2.69/3.11) saves 0.119 x (1 - 0.865) = 0.016 s. Alignment trim cost 1.8% of bytes = 0.002 s. Co-activation chain merges about 19% of reads at cross-prompt (7.54 -> 6.13 reads per layer, C.md); merged reads run 3.42 vs 3.11 GB/s: +0.1 x 0.19... under 0.003 s. Total saving about 0.021 s: 0.289 / 0.268 = 1.08x serial. Overlapped IO and compute makes it about 1.00x (B.md recorded-rates row: 1.00x compute-capped).
- **Cheapest test:** tools/read_bench.py already exists: time 3 parallel 0.9 MB preads at the real gate/up/down offsets vs one 2.86 MB pread of a copied contiguous record, while llama-bench runs 4 compute threads in parallel (N-031 concurrency check). If the single-extent advantage is < 8% under concurrent compute, drop it.
- **Prior art:** SGLang Expert Pack: offline per-(layer, expert) contiguous block, 4096-byte alignment checked, direct I/O https://lmsys.org/blog/2026-08-29-sglang-ssd-expert-pack/ (no A/B vs unpacked layout in the post); llama.cpp RFC #23324 repacked-per-expert disk tool https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/discussions/23324; EStream (group of experts as one extent) https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.06551 ; flash-moe reports expert file clustering gave 0% benefit at about 7 MB granularity (via search summary, not read in full).
- **Skeptic (weakened, corrected 1.06x):** Recomputed from M15 and M23 (notes/Measurements). The headline 1.08x survives only as a serial, idle-disk ceiling. Realistic range is 1.00-1.06x.

1. Baseline is mixed up. M23 gives about 353 MiB per token and 0.29 s per token. The 0.119 s IO share is computed by assuming 3.11 GB/s (370 MB / 3.11 = 0.119 s), which is the rate the idea claims to reach. If the stock three-tensor layout runs at 2.69 GB/s, the baseline IO time is 0.138 s. The saving is then 0.138 - 0.119 = 0.019 s. The proposal instead applies a 13.5% saving to 0.119 s (0.016 s), which is the same order but not the same arithmetic. The pool's actual read rate was never measured, only inferred. The 0.11 s IO share in M23 is also a rough figure.

2. Apples-to-oranges rate comparison. M15 measured 0.9 MB x 4 threads, meaning 4 independent 0.9 MB reads in flight. It did not measure 3 reads per expert. With the pool's 4 reader threads, queue depth is already 4 either way. M15's own note says the three tensors of an expert read in parallel behave like the 0.9 MB x 4 row. Splitting the gap into extent size and queue depth is not done. M15 was also run on the 27B file with an idle machine and no compute running. Under concurrent 4-thread compute the rates will be lower, and the size advantage may shrink. The 3.11 and 2.69 figures differ by 15%, which is within the "shared, noisy" range B.md already flags for its own I/O run.

3. Compute-capped when overlapped. B.md's recorded-rates row shows layout and IO gains collapse to about 1.00x once IO and compute overlap. M23 bounds an overlapped design at 6-8 tok/s, so IO is not the binding term there. Anything that shrinks IO is worth at most 0.02 s of 0.29 s (about 7%) on today's serial pool. For the 125B target the compute ceiling is 4.58 tok/s, and the layout gain would not move it.

4. Chain and coalescing add-on is negligible. C.md shows 19% fewer reads at cross-prompt. M15's 4.0 MB row is 3.42 GB/s versus 3.11 GB/s, a 10% rate gain applied to a fraction of reads, so under 0.003 s. Flash-moe reports expert clustering gave 0% at about 7 MB granularity (the search summary is third-party, from a p.codekk.com mirror, and not read in full). That matches the finding that large extents are already near device saturation. Its regime differs (Apple Silicon unified memory, GPU, 6.75 MB experts), but it does not support the add-on.

5. Prior art contradicts novelty. The layout is already published as SGLang Expert Pack, llama.cpp RFC #23324 and EStream, so it is known. None of the three has an A/B against the unpacked layout in the regime described here (CPU, small RAM, buffered or F_NOCACHE pread). The regime is therefore untested rather than contradicted. I did not read the SGLang, RFC or EStream pages myself, and the flash-moe summary is secondhand.

6. Other costs. A one-time offline repack doubles disk space or needs a custom file format, so stock GGUF compatibility is lost. Slot padding up to 4 KiB multiples is already satisfied, so the claimed alignment gain is small (1.8% of bytes, about 0.002 s).

Net: 0.289 / (0.289 - 0.019) = 1.07x best case serial. Subtract noise and contention under compute and the figure is about 1.03-1.06x serial and about 1.00x overlapped. It is cheap and harmless, but not a headline lever.
  - missed prior art: 
  - decisive test: Run tools/read_bench.py with the real gate/up/down offsets of the Qwen3-30B file, using F_NOCACHE and 4 reader threads, while llama-bench runs 4 compute threads. Compare three 0.9-1.3 MB preads per expert against one 2.86 MB pread of a copied contiguous record, on the same expert sequence from the trace. Measure end-to-end tok/s of the pool with the repacked file as well, since IO under compute contention is what matters. If the single-extent advantage is under 8% in GB/s, or under 3% in tok/s, drop the idea.

### layout #3: Per-layer tensor-type map: demote the Q6_K down projections (24 of 48 layers) and move down to Q3_K where sensitivity allows
- **Gap:** GGUF header parse of Qwen3-30B-A3B-Q4_K_M: ffn_down_exps is Q6_K in 24 layers (0-5, 8, 11, 14, ..., 41-47) and Q4_K in the other 24; gate and up are Q4_K everywhere. This is llama.cpp's fixed 'use_more_bits' heuristic, not measured sensitivity. Expert = 3.06 MB in Q6_K-down layers, 2.65 MB otherwise (mean 2.86). Reads are 129 miss-experts x 2.86 MB = 353 MiB per token (M23). M16: existing low-bit kernels do not speed decode (Q3_K_S 0.86x, Q2_K 1.12x), so only the byte saving counts. M17 upper bounds (OLMoE, requantised from Q4_K_M): Q3_K_S +10.4% ppl, Q2_K +26% ppl, Q8_0 +0.2%.
- **Change:** Use llama-quantize --tensor-type-file to build a per-layer map: step 1 Q6_K down -> Q4_K in layers measured insensitive; step 2 down -> Q3_K (3.44 bpw) in the least sensitive layers, chosen by per-layer KL against a Q8_0 reference with an imatrix. Sensitivity from layer-by-layer ablation of one layer at a time (48 short perplexity runs, or a gradient proxy).
- **Bound:** Step 1: about 64.7 miss-experts per token fall in Q6_K layers, each saves 405,504 B -> 26.2 MB of 370 MB (7.1%) -> IO 0.119 s x 0.071 = 0.0084 s -> 0.289/0.2806 = 1.03x. Step 2 (all downs to Q3_K = 676 KB vs mean 1,087,488 B): expert 2.86 -> 2.445 MB (-14.5%), IO -0.017 s, plus a larger pool in experts (+17%, hit 66% -> about 70%, estimate) -> about -0.025 s -> 1.09x, minus the Q3 dequant penalty (M16 decode 0.86x on the Q3_K_S file) on one third of expert FLOPs, net about 1.06x. At 125B/16 GB (IO 0.097 of 0.315 s): -14.5% bytes -> 0.301 s = 1.05x. All serial-model estimates.
- **Cheapest test:** Local, in RAM: requantise with --allow-requantize to the map; measure (a) decode tok/s fully cached for Q6_K-down vs Q4_K-down (is the Q6_K kernel part of the 0.06 s over the compute ceiling?), (b) KL/ppl vs a Q8_0 reference, 24 chunks x 512 tokens as in M17. Needs Q8_0 reference (O-061 says a Q4_K_M reference can only return NO_WIN). Kill if step 1 costs > 1% ppl or decode does not speed up.
- **Prior art:** MoPEQ per-expert Hessian-trace bit allocation https://arxiv.org/html/2509.02512v1 ; MxMoE mixed-precision with sensitivity and activation frequency https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.05799 ; DynaExq runtime hotness-driven precision (GPU, HBM budget) https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.15015 ; HOBBIT low-bit substitution on misses. The search found no work that optimises bit-width against SSD miss bytes on CPU. Our own A.md family #7 (mixed 2/3/4-bit) found small gain at 8 GB, about nothing above.

### layout #4: Allocate bits by miss mass, not by activation frequency (cold-expert low-bit tier)
- **Gap:** Hypothesis in notes (M12, D.md P-0184): hot experts are cached, so cold experts dominate disk bytes and can be stored cheaper. Trace test on the 3 real traces (global LRU of 2098 experts = 6000 MB; rank on the two other prompts): the coldest half of experts per layer gets 11.6%/29.9%/20.4% of accesses (db/story/code) but only 26%/33%/34% of misses (miss share by train-rank quartile db [0.336,0.402,0.189,0.073], story [0.338,0.335,0.195,0.132], code [0.317,0.347,0.206,0.130]). With in-sample ranking the cold half is only 11%/8%/17% of misses (oracle quartiles [0.377,0.513,0.108,0.002] for db). So misses are mostly warm experts, not cold ones, and the cold tail is unstable across prompts (M12).
- **Change:** Per-expert two-tier file: experts in the bottom half of a cross-prompt frequency ranking stored at Q2_K/Q3_K (2.56-3.44 bpw), the rest at the original type; the pool holds them at their stored size.
- **Bound:** Cold half = 0.26..0.34 of miss bytes, take 0.30. Q2_K saves 1 - 2.5625/4.85 = 47% of those bytes: miss bytes -14%. IO 0.119 s x 0.14 = 0.0167 s saved; 0.289/0.272 = 1.06x serial. Quality: cold half receives 12-30% of accesses, Q2_K upper-bound loss +26% ppl (M17) applied to that share gives roughly +3..+8% ppl, above the 5% kill line in the worst case.
- **Cheapest test:** Free: the bound above is already trace-computed. Decisive step: extend scratchpad angle_layout/t.py to 16 and 32 GB-equivalent caches and to prompt-specific ranks; if cold-half miss share stays < 40% at every size, close it. Only then spend a perplexity run.
- **Prior art:** DynaExq hotness-driven precision https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.15015 (GPU, runtime, high-precision resident set); GEMQ static gradient-based allocation (D.md P-0184); MoPEQ https://arxiv.org/html/2509.02512v1 . None of them weights by cache-miss mass on a disk-backed pool.

### layout #5: Cross-layer successor bundling in the file (expert (L,e) stored next to its best next-layer partner)
- **Gap:** M14: a layer-L-1 to layer-L co-occurrence predictor reaches precision@8 of 0.51-0.55, so a layout that puts the predicted successor adjacent could merge two reads into one 5.7 MB extent (rate 3.42-3.44 GB/s vs 3.11). Trace test (angle_layout/u.py, global LRU 2098 experts, best-successor table from the other two prompts, or from the first half of the same prompt): the fraction of misses at layer L+1 whose expert is the stored successor of a missed expert at layer L in the same token is 0.083/0.085/0.081 cross-prompt (db/story/code) and 0.093/0.125/0.079 same-prompt.
- **Change:** Interleave a second dimension in the file order: chains of (layer, expert) pairs along the layer axis, so a miss read pulls the likely next-layer partner in the same request.
- **Bound:** At most 8-12% of misses can be merged. A merged pair runs at 3.42 vs 3.11 GB/s (+10% rate on those bytes) and also saves one request: IO saving <= 0.10 x 0.10 x 0.119 s = 0.0012 s of 0.289 s = 1.004x. Speculative bundling of non-needed partners would add wasted bytes (1 - 0.55 precision at best), which cost more than the saving.
- **Cheapest test:** Done (trace): coalescible share 8-12%. Stop here unless a real expert-id-to-partner structure appears on the 512-expert model's traces (open item 2 of the handoff).
- **Prior art:** ExpertFlow / pre-gated and cross-layer prefetchers (prefetch, not file layout); LLM-in-a-flash row-column and co-activation bundling (neuron level, within a layer); flash-moe reports clustering gave 0% (search summary). Cross-layer bundling of experts within the file is not found in the searches.

### layout #6: Hot-first split file / cold-start seed region
- **Gap:** M22 pool starts cold each process; M12 says a hot set transfers poorly across prompts; VPS pinning (row 5 of COMPARISON.md) cost about 60 s of load and gave no net gain on long runs. Trace test: seed the LRU with the top-N experts ranked on the other two prompts and replay the first T tokens. N=1000: db T=32 misses 2802 -> 2179 (saves 623, i.e. 0.62 per seeded expert), story 2519 -> 2019 (0.50), code 3160 -> 2602 (0.56); T=249 saves the same absolute count (one-time seed benefit). IO seconds (misses x 2.86 MB at 3.11 GB/s plus seed read at an optimistic 3.4 GB/s sequential): db 2.6 -> 2.8 s over 32 tokens, 8.1 -> 8.3 s over 249 tokens; story 6.6 -> 7.0; code 11.9 -> 12.2.
- **Change:** Reorder the expert file so the cross-prompt top experts per layer are one contiguous region read sequentially at load into the pool.
- **Bound:** Each seeded expert saves 0.50-0.62 misses on average (< 1.0), and a sequential read is at best 3.42 / 3.11 = 1.10x faster than scattered misses, so break-even needs saved/seeded > 0.91; measured 0.50-0.62 < 0.91. Net IO change is -3% to -6% (a slowdown). Bound 1.00x with this SSD.
- **Cheapest test:** Already done on traces (angle_layout/v.py). A decisive extra check is a sequential 1 GB read rate on the Mac internal NVMe: only if it exceeds about 5.5 GB/s (it is 3.4-3.5 for 4 MB reads, M15) does the idea reopen.
- **Prior art:** Pinning hot experts: VPS pin A/B in our notes (M13), PowerInfer-style hot/cold split is GPU-resident; EDGEO keeps per-layer LRU hot experts https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.18063 (search summary only). No published win for cold-start seeding on CPU+NVMe.


## Angle: redteam

RED-TEAM FINDINGS (ranked by effect on headlines). All sim numbers are mine, from tinyram-llm data/traces (Qwen3-30B, 128 experts top-8, 48 layers, 384 entries/token, about 2.65 MB/entry); scratch at <scratch> and e.py.

1. The 3.46 tok/s pool figure (M23) is a cold-start number. LRU on the real traces from an empty cache gives hit 0.66/0.71/0.66 over the first 16 tokens at a 2264-entry pool (about 6 GB), matching the measured 66%. The same cache after warm-up gives 0.95/0.98/0.91. Misses fall from about 0.34 to about 0.05 of 384 entries, so read time falls from about 0.11 s to about 0.02 s per token. Compute plus sync is about 0.18 s, so steady state is about 5 tok/s (estimate), roughly 1.45x above what we quote. The side effect is that every 8-16 token run understates long-generation speed, and the cold-start term also contaminates the 5x (M22). At the 1500 MB pool (566 entries) cold8 is 0.31/0.42/0.37 and steady is 0.43-0.48, so the 5x there is only mildly biased. This also means M22/M23 hit rates are not inflated by llama-bench's fixed synthetic input (L07 worry): the measured 66% equals the real-trace cold-16 sim.

2. Time budget of the 5x run does not add up unless the residual mmap paging is large. M22 token time is 1/1.72 = 0.58 s. Compute is about 0.18 s (M23). Pool misses at 566 entries and 45% hit are about 0.55 x 1.0 GB = 0.55 GB, about 0.18 s at 3.1 GB/s. The remaining about 0.2 s matches 0.065 GiB/token still paged through mmap at 0.28-0.6 GB/s. That is a third of the token time spent in the slow path we claim to have replaced (estimate; needs per-tensor mincore).

3. The 5x baseline is stock macOS mmap, a weak baseline. Combined effect of read size, policy and pool is credited to the pool. The fair comparison (pool vs mmap plus per-expert MADV_WILLNEED/F_RDADVISE at 2.7 MB) was never run on the Mac. Linux readahead and real small-RAM PCs behave differently (VPS: 0.04-0.18 tok/s, M18/M20). Prior RFC data: 0.43 -> 0.66 t/s from a persistent read pool (about 1.5x) on a 73.5 GB model with 32 GB RAM, much less than 5x (https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/discussions/23324). The 5x is a ballast-regime, macOS-stock figure; expect 2-5x elsewhere. Ballast realism: a wired 9 GB ballast is not compressible, not swappable and leaves no competing page cache; a real 8 GB PC runs a browser and OS caches (M21 notes swap +1 GB). The 10 GB ballast failed to wire, so the cap curve has one valid point.

4. madvise 1.65x (M20): one throttled VPS disk at 120 MiB/s, one prompt, n=5, 2 threads, fully I/O-bound. Null pair is clean (0.2%), so it is real for that regime, but the gain is I/O-share dependent; on a 3 GB/s NVMe with 0.18 s compute the same prefetch should give much less, and the Mac prefetch A/B at ballast is still absent. Do not carry 1.65x to the Mac or to the 125B.

5. Hit curves: the 125B has 512 experts, top-10, so footprint is 480 entries (about 1.3 GB), not 384, and reuse is lower (public benchmark: 0.77 at 16 GB vs our 0.95). The combination table D is evaluated at the 0.95 point, where overlap looks like 1.03-1.04x. At hit 0.77 and 16 GB: io = 0.23 x 1.30 GB / 3.1 = 0.096 s, compute 1/4.58 = 0.218 s, serial 0.314 s -> overlap 0.218 s = 1.44x. So the lever ranking changes with the hit curve: overlap and prefetch were dismissed on the wrong hit rate. Cross-layer prefetch 1.00-1.075x was also measured in the 0.95 regime. A RFC commenter measured 1.30x from pipelining next-layer misses at 75% hit on an M1 Air CPU.

6. Reading the compute ceiling as compute is likely wrong. 30B: 1.86 GB touched x 8.3 tok/s = 15.4 GB/s. 125B: 3.23 GB x 4.57 = 14.8 GB/s. Two models, same 15 GB/s, while 55-75 GFLOP/s is a small fraction of AVX512 VNNI peak. That is a DRAM-streaming ceiling (LPDDR4X-3733 4x16 peak is about 58 GB/s, to be measured). Hardware can do more than software achieves; nobody tested a read-bandwidth microbenchmark.

7. Quality gates: top-6 +4.7% and top-4 +30.9% on Qwen3-30B (few chunks, plain renormalisation) vs OLMoE +8.8/+45.6% vs a 397B paper with anchored denominator 0.55 MMLU at 10->5. Three different answers; none on the 125B. Measured on few chunks, so confidence intervals are not stated. Cheapest decisive test: same perplexity run on 5+ chunk sets with bootstrap CI plus anchored denominator; then a downstream task (GSM8K 100 items).

8. Order effects: M22 notes both arms trend upward across pairs (OS state), and 3 pairs only. Use ABBA with a null (pool vs pool) pair and an n >= 5, 32+ token real-prompt run. Single model, single prompt family: traces db/story/code are 249-299 tokens each; cross-prompt cold warm-start sim gives first-16 hit +0 to +0.2 depending on cache size.

DISMISSED TOO EARLY or mis-scored: (a) overlap/prefetch lever (scored at hit 0.95; at 0.77 it is 1.3-1.44x); (b) pool size under ballast (never swept under ballast; M23 was unballasted); (c) prefill path (pool measured on decode only; prefill touches 68-93% of experts, so a 69 GB file is read once per prompt); (d) 8-thread collapse treated as fixed (it is spin barriers, SMT might give 5-15%).

CONFIRMED DEAD (with my numbers): batching independent sequences to share expert reads: union of 2 and 3 different-prompt tokens is 362 and 343 entries per token vs 384 (only 6-11% sharing) and LRU hit falls from 0.46 to 0.05 at 566 entries and 0.69 to 0.50-0.61 at 1130; lossless entropy coding of Q4_K expert bytes: 8 MB samples of OLMoE Q4_K_M give zlib 0.981-0.998, lzma 0.987-1.000, byte entropy 7.80-7.93 bits, nibble entropy 3.91-3.97 bits; the I/O saving is at most about 2% (consistent with EntroLLM's note that block-wise quantization has high entropy).

### redteam #1: Prefill as a layer-streamed pipeline (sequential layer reads double-buffered against batch compute)
- **Gap:** Pool and pool tuning (M22, M23) measured decode only. Prefill touches 68-93% of experts (DEEP_SUMMARY item 4: 57% at 84 tokens, 68% at 228 on Qwen3-30B, 91-93% OLMoE), i.e. up to the whole 69 GB file per prompt. VPS wall split (M20): load plus prompt eval 181 s of 702 s, tok/s hides it. Time to first token for the 125B is not in any headline.
- **Change:** In prefill, read each layer's touched experts with large sequential reads in layer order, prefetching layer L+1 into the pool while layer L's batched matmuls run (prefill compute is batch-bound, so overlap is near perfect). No routing prediction needed beyond a union over the prompt chunk.
- **Bound:** 200-token prompt on 125B: reads 85% of 69 GB = 59 GB / 3.1 GB/s = 19 s; compute 200 x 12 GFLOP / about 150 GFLOP/s (estimate) = 16 s. Serial 35 s -> overlapped 19 s = 1.8x over a serial pool path; over stock mmap (0.34 GB/s: 173 s) it is far larger. For 2000-token prompts compute-bound: 1.14x.
- **Cheapest test:** On the Mac with 9 GB ballast and the pool patch, llama-bench -p 64 -n 0 and -p 256 -n 0 vs mmap; use the prefill-trace patch to log time per layer and bytes read.
- **Prior art:** Layer-wise weight streaming for prefill is standard in GPU offload (FlexGen-style, KTransformers prefill); on NVMe-backed CPU with tiny RAM it is not documented in the sources we searched.
- **Skeptic (weakened, corrected 1.50x):** The arithmetic checks out, but the 1.8x is a best case, and it is only a time-to-first-token (TTFT) gain over a serial pool path, not a throughput gain.

1. Overlap can never beat 2x. Serial time is R+C and overlapped time is max(R,C), so the gain is (R+C)/max(R,C) <= 2. The proposal's 1.8x needs read time R and compute time C to be almost equal (19 s vs 16 s). That equality rests on an unmeasured compute rate of "about 150 GFLOP/s (estimate)".
   - Measured decode ceiling is 55 GFLOP/s, and no prefill GFLOP/s figure exists in our files.
   - DEEP_SUMMARY says CPU prefill takes about 19-21 s per 500 tokens. That is about 25 tok/s. At 12 GFLOP/token (125B) it is about 300 GFLOP/s, and at about 6.6 GFLOP/token (the Qwen3-30B proxy) it is about 165 GFLOP/s. I could not tell from the file which model that figure refers to.
   - If compute is about 300 GFLOP/s, 200 tokens on the 125B cost 8 s. The result is then 27 s serial vs 19 s overlapped, or 1.42x.
   - If compute is about 100 GFLOP/s, it is 24 s. The result is then 43 s vs 24 s, or 1.8x.
   - Sweeping C from 8 to 24 s gives 1.4x to 1.8x before any losses.

2. The overlap is not free on 4 cores. The pread copies and the matmuls share cores and memory bandwidth, and 4 threads are already best (8 threads collapse to 0.26 tok/s). Losing about one core to I/O raises C by about 25%. Realistic overlap efficiency is 70-80%, which gives about 1.3-1.5x.

3. The proxy test is compute-bound. Qwen3-30B has about 3.3B active parameters, so about 6.6 GFLOP/token.
   - A 200-token prompt costs 1.3 TFLOP, which is about 8.8 s at 150 GFLOP/s.
   - It reads 0.68 x 18.6 GB = 12.6 GB, which is about 4 s at 3.1 GB/s.
   - Serial time is 12.8 s and overlapped time is 8.8 s, so the gain is 1.45x at best.
   - The proxy therefore cannot show the 125B regime. It will under-read the benefit, and the 125B number stays an extrapolation.

4. The coverage figure of 85% is extrapolated. Measured coverage is 57-68% on the 128-expert model at 84-228 tokens, and the 512-expert case is marked "unmeasured" in DEEP_SUMMARY. Lower coverage shrinks R and pushes the workload toward compute-bound.

5. The baseline is inflated. The 1.8x is over a "serial pool path" that may not exist. llama.cpp already evaluates the graph layer by layer. With the explicit pool, misses within a layer are likely fetched at that layer, so the real baseline is not known. The comparison over stock mmap (173 s) is the pool's gain and should not be credited to this idea.

6. Long prompts gain nothing from this and get slightly worse in total reads. The proposal's own figure for 2000 tokens is 1.14x. With a 512-token ubatch, each ubatch re-streams the touched experts, so a 2000-token prompt reads about 4 x 59 GB = 236 GB unless the batch size is raised. That cost is hidden only while compute dominates.

7. Prior art exists, so "variant" overstates novelty.
   - Edge0 (arXiv 2609.18063, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.18063, 35B MoE streamed from SSD on a Mac mini) loads whole layers during prefill and overlaps that load with the previous layer's work. The search summary also says prediction matters less in prefill. Our own B.md already cites Edge0. I could not read the PDF body, so I have no Edge0 timing numbers.
   - DoubleWord FlashOffload (https://blog.doubleword.ai/flashoffload) uses double-buffered per-layer prefetch for prefill, and the vLLM RFC (https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/issues/33869) proposes a double-buffered prefetch mode for large prefill batches. Both are GPU regimes where PCIe is much faster than NVMe.
   - A Jetson Orin Nano write-up (https://dev.to/anyesh/running-a-26b-moe-on-an-8-gb-jetson-by-streaming-experts-from-ssd-2jpf) reports cold prefill at about a quarter of the in-memory speed. That is a counterpoint to near-perfect hiding.
   - Flash-MoE (397B on a 48 GB Mac with pread) is also cited in DEEP_SUMMARY.

8. The scope is TTFT only. Decode tok/s, which carries the headline numbers, is unchanged. No quality risk was found.

Corrected bound: about 1.5x on 125B prefill time for prompts up to about 500 tokens, with a plausible range of 1.2-1.8x. It is about 1.2-1.45x on the proxy, and about 1.0-1.14x for prompts of 2000 tokens or more.
  - missed prior art: Edge0 (arXiv 2609.18063) loads whole layers during prefill and overlaps that load with the previous layer's work, on SSD with a Mac. DoubleWord FlashOffload (https://blog.doubleword.ai/flashoffload) and the vLLM CPU-offload RFC (https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/issues/33869) both use double-buffered per-layer prefetch for large prefill batches, in GPU regimes. Flash-MoE (397B from flash with pread) streams a 125B-class model with explicit reads.
  - decisive test: Run two measurements on the Mac with the pool patch, with the 9 GB ballast in place and a cold cache before each run. First, measure compute-only prefill rate: llama-bench -p 256 -n 0 with the whole model resident (no ballast), giving the real C in GFLOP/s. Second, run the same -p 64 and -p 256 under ballast with the pool. Log per-layer wall time, bytes read and read-wait time with the prefill-trace patch. Compute the overlap ceiling as (R+C)/max(R,C) from the measured R and C, and compare it with the measured pool-vs-pool-with-layer-prefetch gain. If the proxy gain at -p 256 is under about 1.2x, the 125B claim does not hold.

### redteam #2: Move the residual mmap paging of non-expert tensors into locked or explicitly read memory
- **Gap:** M22: pool arm still pages 0.06-0.07 GiB/token through mmap (rate 0.28-0.60 GB/s from M15/Playbook row 6) = 0.11-0.25 s of a 0.58 s token (1.72 tok/s). Token budget check: 0.18 s compute (M23) + about 0.18 s pool misses (566 entries, hit about 0.45, 0.55 GB at 3.1 GB/s) + about 0.2 s mmap = 0.56 s, matches 0.58 measured.
- **Change:** Load attention, router, embedding, output and norm tensors (about 1.2-1.5 GB on the 30B, pure dense, always touched) with plain read into anonymous memory or mlock them after one sequential read, so only the expert pool is subject to eviction. Tiny patch next to the existing pool code; no output change.
- **Bound:** 0.58 s/token now. Remove 0.11-0.25 s mmap paging -> 0.33-0.47 s -> 1.23x-1.76x. Midpoint 1.5, bound quoted 1.6. Estimate; depends on whether the paged bytes are really non-expert tensors.
- **Cheapest test:** With 9 GB ballast and the existing pool, snapshot mincore per tensor class (expert vs non-expert) before/after 32 tokens, then run the pool with a pre-read plus mlock of non-expert tensors (about 1.5 GB) and compare tok/s ABBA, n=5, real prompt, 32+ tokens.
- **Prior art:** Standard practice (llama.cpp --mlock, offload non-experts to resident memory in KTransformers-style CPU/GPU splits); the RFC https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/discussions/23324 keeps non-expert weights resident and pools only experts. Nobody reports it as the residual term under a RAM cap.
- **Skeptic (weakened, corrected 1.30x):** Arithmetic recomputed from M22 and M23 (the only files I read). The bound of 1.6x is not supported, for these reasons.

1. The residual is probably not steady state. M22 gives 0.06-0.07 GiB paged per token, from an 8-token run where the pool starts cold in each process. That is about 0.5 GiB over the whole run, against 1.2-1.5 GB of non-expert tensors. It looks like a one-time cold fill of part of the dense tensors, amortised over only 8 tokens. M22 never says what fraction is steady state. The proposal's own caveat, "depends on whether the paged bytes are really non-expert", is the main uncertainty, and nothing in our files breaks the 0.06-0.07 GiB down by tensor class.

2. The 0.11-0.25 s figure applies the wrong rate. It multiplies about 0.07 GB by 0.28-0.60 GB/s, which are rates for plain mmap on the file, measured under mmap thrash with 0.68-1.14 GiB per token paged. Hot dense tensors are touched every token and read as large contiguous runs, so they should be the last pages the OS evicts. At the 3.1 GB/s explicit rate, 0.07 GB costs only about 0.02 s, so the saving would be about 1.04x. Only the slowest assumed rate gives 0.25 s.

3. The token budget is circular. 0.18 s of compute comes from M23's unballasted token, which is 0.29 s minus 0.11 s of reads. Under ballast the hit rate of 0.45 and the 0.18 s of pool misses are assumed, not measured. M23 measured 61% hit at 3000 MB and 66% at 6000 MB, and the 1500 MB pool was never logged. Then 0.20 s of mmap is just whatever is left to reach 0.58 s, so the fit proves nothing. If the miss cost is larger or compute is slower under ballast, the mmap share shrinks.

4. Memory is taken from the pool. Under a 9 GB wired ballast the pool is only 1500 MB. Locking 1.2-1.5 GB of dense tensors either doubles the memory in use or takes about as much as the whole pool. The proposal's cost of 0.04-0.06 hit (about 0.02 s) is too small. Misses cost about 0.19 s at 45% hit, so losing 0.05 hit adds roughly 0.02-0.03 s, and a larger loss costs more. macOS also limits mlock, and the 9 GB wired ballast leaves about 4 GB for the OS, so the lock may fail or provoke compressor and swap activity.

5. Prior art confirms the idea is standard, so it adds nothing new. llama.cpp documents --mlock and --no-mmap. FlexInfer (https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.03777; I read only the abstract, as the PDF was unreadable) uses flexible tensor preservation and balanced memory locking, and reports up to 12.5x over mmap-based offloading. That 12.5x is against a baseline where almost every weight access faults, not against a pool that already handles the experts, so it does not support 1.6x here. A web search found no controlled benchmark of mlock for dense tensors against expert pooling under a RAM cap, so none contradicts the bound and none supports it. It also notes that mlock only helps when what is locked fits in RAM.

6. Corrected bound. Optimistic case: 0.20 s of steady paging removed, 0.58 to 0.38 s, 1.5x. ### redteam #3: Hit-first compute with asynchronous miss reads inside each layer, plus next-layer miss pipelining
- **Gap:** M23: reads about 0.11 s and compute plus sync about 0.18 s per token, serial (0.29 s, 3.46 tok/s at 66% hit). Projection at the real 125B hit 0.77, 16 GB: read 0.096 s, compute 0.218 s, serial 0.314 s = 3.18 tok/s; lever table D credits overlap only 1.03-1.04x because it used hit 0.95.

- **Bound:** 30B at 6 GB pool: 1/max(0.18, 0.11) vs 1/0.29 = 5.55/3.45 = 1.6x. 125B at 16 GB hit 0.77: 0.218 vs 0.314 s = 1.44x. In-layer hiding alone is limited to min(layer read 2.0 ms, resident-expert compute about 2.7 ms of 4.5 ms layer) so most of it is hideable; realistic 1.25-1.4x.
- **Cheapest test:** Add layer-wise timers (phase timing patch exists: F33) to measure per-layer miss-read time vs resident-expert compute on the 6 GB pool; then trial overlap on the 566-entry pool at 9 GB ballast. Decisive: does tok/s rise from 1.72 toward 1/max(compute, read)?

### redteam #8: Fix the 8-thread collapse so SMT siblings can serve I/O and part of compute
- **Gap:** M23: 4 threads 2.98 tok/s, 2 threads 2.74, 8 threads 0.258 (11x collapse, ggml barriers spin). The i7 has 8 hardware threads but only 4 usable; the 2 vs 4 thread difference is only 9% so compute is bandwidth-limited, and extra threads only matter if they fix latency, not throughput.
- **Change:** Keep 4 compute threads pinned one per physical core; run pool I/O threads on the SMT siblings with blocking waits (no spin) at lower priority; replace pure spin barriers with spin-then-sleep when threads exceed cores.
- **Bound:** Compute scales 2->4 threads by 1.09x only, so SMT compute gain bounded by about 1.05x; main gain would be removing the I/O thread stalls from the 4 compute threads (read 0.11 s of 0.29 s may partly be CPU-side memcpy/page-in). Upper bound if all I/O CPU cost is hidden: 0.29 -> 0.25 s = 1.15x. Estimate.
- **Cheapest test:** Pin 4 compute threads via thread_policy affinity tags (macOS) and run the pool I/O workers on separate threads; compare to 4 unpinned. Log CPU time of the I/O workers (getrusage) to see whether reads even cost CPU (F_NOCACHE DMA suggests not): if under 5% the idea dies.
- **Prior art:** Standard llama.cpp guidance (-t = physical cores); ggml spin/poll threadpool (--poll flag) documented in llama.cpp; no MoE-offload-specific source found.
